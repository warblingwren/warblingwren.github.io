About Warbling Wren

Hi, I'm Matt, creator of Warbling Wren - The Musician's Companion.

I'm a music fan. And when I'm not making music, I'm a computer scientist and Infosec Jerk.

My musical journey

My musical journey started in my mid-teens with an acoustic guitar that was gifted to me by a relative.

My earliest musical memories are classic country that I heard in my grandparents' kitchen: Waylon Jennings, George Strait, Merle Haggard. I later heard this type of music called "Outlaw Country", and wondered what they had all done that got everyone so upset.

Some of my musical influence came from the places I ended up, like construction job sites where the playlist was late '70s and early '80s rock records (what we now call classic rock), like Tom Petty, Warren Zevon and Joe Cocker, along with folk from Jim Croce, James Taylor and Harry Chapin. I hit my teenage years right as grunge arrived, and I gravitated toward Nirvana, Pearl Jam, Silverchair and Alice in Chains. Since then I've been all over the map: country, rock, folk and jazz.

I've always been fascinated with the way music unites people. Whether it's a stadium of fans all singing the same lyrics in unison, or a couple of people in the office who find themselves humming the same tune.

Music is a powerful adhesive.

Why I built Warbling Wren

I played pretty consistently into my mid-30s. Then life got busy, my playing hit a plateau and I put my musical journey on hold for a long time.

Looking back, my first run at music ended because I'd stopped making progress, and I had a hunch about why. I had never really learned music. I'd memorized chords and riffs without understanding why they worked. When it came to improvising or playing with someone else, I was stuck. So I stopped.

In my musical second life, I picked the guitar back up, and I also started learning piano. That's where I hit the first snag that led to Warbling Wren. There are plenty of guitar tab sites, but very few show piano chords and guitar chords together.

I also knew I was going to need a tool that would get me over those doldrums of complacency and push my playing and musical journey to each new level.

Turns out, music theory is the solution to both of these problems... but that led to a new problem: music theory is not that much fun, and it's difficult to understand. I wanted an app that made theory fun to learn and helped me uncover patterns I could apply all over the keyboard and the fretboard. And I wanted the one ultimate musical reference to go with it. Warbling Wren is both.

Warbling Wren is designed to help every musician at every stage, whether you're learning your first chord pattern or composing your masterpiece. Warbling Wren will guide you on a new musical adventure with every click.

What's in a Name?

I love to listen to the birds sing. They communicate exclusively in song. From the largest to the smallest, every bird has a voice, and their songs are unique to the flock, filled with meaning and (bonus) delightful to the listener.

What Warbling Wren does

The interactive app

  • A full 88-key piano. Every chord is shown on the keyboard from A0 to C8 and played with real piano samples. Colors tell you which octave a note is in and what role it plays in the chord: the root is darkest, then the 3rd and 5th, with the 7th lightest.
  • A 24-fret guitar fretboard. The same chord appears on the neck in the same colors, and you hear it strummed. It works in 16 tunings, including Standard, Drop D, DADGAD, the open tunings and New Standard.
  • Scales on the fretboard. Every fretboard layout is a scale. The notes of the key are mapped across all 24 frets, and each dot is shaded by its scale degree, with the tonic darkest. Pick a chord and a progression, and the neck shows you the scale pattern behind it, everywhere it occurs.
  • Every chord, in every key. Choose from 14 chord types: major, minor, dominant 7th, major 7th, minor 7th, major 6th, minor 6th, suspended 2nd and 4th, diminished, diminished 7th, half-diminished, augmented and augmented 7th. Each one is available on all 12 roots.
  • Chord progressions by genre. Pick from progressions ranging from Blues, Jazz and Country to Rock & Roll, Folk and Pop, and see them played out in the key of the chord you chose.
  • The relative key, side by side. Every chord shows its relative major or minor, with the same progression in that key.
  • The circle of fifths. See where your key and its relative sit on the circle, and how the chords in a progression relate to each other.
  • Voicings. Play a chord in any octave on the piano. Step through playable guitar shapes from the nut up the neck.
  • Sharing. Every custom voicing or chord progression has a link and a QR code that open exactly what you're looking at (chord, progression, tuning and voicings), so you can easily share it with other players and band members. Use the share button to copy the link or show the QR code.

The chord reference

  • 290+ reference pages. One page for every chord type and progression combination: 14 chord types × 21 progressions.
  • Every root on one page. Each page plays the progression from all 12 roots. For each root you get the chord badges, the chord tones (for both guitar and piano), a piano keyboard, a first-position guitar chord diagram, the relative key and the circle of fifths.
  • Fast and simple. The reference pages are plain pages that load quickly and print cleanly, with a convenient share feature.
  • Customize Voicing. Every section in the reference links to the same chord and progression in the interactive app, where you can hear it, change the voicing and share it.

How to use Warbling Wren

The interactive app

  1. Pick a chord. Choose a chord type from the dropdown, then tap a root. The chord lights up on the piano and the guitar, and you hear it played.
  2. Listen to the parts. Tap a tone circle to hear that one note and see everywhere it appears on the piano keyboard and the guitar fretboard.
  3. Change the octave or the shape. On the piano, pick an octave row to hear the chord higher or lower. On the guitar, slide through the playable shapes for each octave.
  4. Choose a progression. Pick a genre and a progression. Each chord in it gets its own music theory breakdown: tap a chord badge to hear it, or a tone to hear a single note.
  5. Read the scale. Look at the fretboard: every dot belongs to the key's scale, shaded by degree (1 darkest, 7 lightest), with a ring on the root. Follow the patterns up and down the neck to practice the scale in every position.
  6. Explore the relative key. The relative panel plays the same progression in the relative major or minor, so you can hear how the two keys relate.
  7. Retune the guitar. Switch tunings and every chord shape is recalculated for the new strings.
  8. Share it. Copy the link from the address bar or use the share button. Whoever opens it sees and hears exactly what you set up.

The chord reference

  1. Open the menu. The chord reference menu is grouped by genre. Open a progression to see its 14 chord types.
  2. Pick a page. Each page covers one progression with one chord type, played from all 12 roots.
  3. Jump to your key. Use the contents at the top of the page to jump straight to the root you want.
  4. Read across. For each chord you get the chord name, its tones, where to put your fingers on the piano, and a guitar shape. The relative key and the circle of fifths sit alongside.
  5. Take it further. Tap Customize Voicing to open that section in the interactive app.

FAQ

Do I need to know music theory to use Warbling Wren?

No. Pick a chord and look and listen. The colors, the scales, the progressions and the circle of fifths show you how it all fits together, and the theory comes along with it.

I only play guitar (or only piano). Is it still useful for me?

Yes. Each instrument works on its own. Seeing both side by side is a bonus: it's one of the quickest ways to understand why a chord is built the way it is.

What do the colors mean?

Each octave has its own color family, so you can see where a note sits on the keyboard or the neck. Within a chord, the shade shows the note's role: the root is darkest, then the 3rd, the 5th and, lightest, the 7th (or 6th). The piano and the guitar use the same colors, so the same note looks the same on both.

What do the Roman numerals mean?

They show each chord's position in the key. Uppercase (I, IV, V) is a major chord, lowercase (ii, vi) is minor, and ° marks a diminished chord. Because the numerals describe positions rather than specific chords, a progression like I–IV–V works in any key. That's why Warbling Wren can play every progression from all 12 roots.

Where are the scales?

Scales are displayed on the guitar fretboard and the piano keyboard. Every layout is a scale: the notes of the key are shown across the whole keyboard and neck, shaded by scale degree, with the tonic darkest and a ring on the root. Pick a chord and a progression, and you're looking at the scale those chords come from. Switch the guitar tuning and the scale pattern moves with it.

What is a relative key?

Every major key shares its notes with one minor key, and vice versa. C major and A minor are relatives. Their chords overlap, which is why songs often move between them so smoothly.

What is the circle of fifths, and how do I read it?

It arranges all 12 keys so that each step around the circle is a fifth apart. Keys next to each other share most of their notes. Major keys sit on the outer ring and their relative minors on the inner ring. Chords that sit close together on the circle tend to sound good together.

A circle of fifths layout is included with every Warbling Wren chord reference.

Why are some notes written as sharps and others as flats?

Notes are spelled the way the key spells them. F major uses B♭, not A♯, and E major uses G♯, not A♭. Spelling notes correctly makes it easier to read music and to see the patterns.

Can I use alternate guitar tunings?

Yes. There are 16 tunings, including Drop D, E♭ Standard, DADGAD, the open tunings and New Standard. Every guitar chord shape is recalculated for the tuning you pick, and only playable shapes are shown.

Why do some guitar shapes go past the 4th fret?

The guitar diagram shows the first playable shape for that chord. Some chords don't have a comfortable shape near the nut in a given tuning, so the diagram moves up the neck to the first one you can actually play.

Can I share what I'm looking at?

Yes. The address bar always holds a link to your current chord, progression, tuning and voicings; simply copy and paste it. There is also a share button for easy QR code sharing. Anyone who opens the link gets the same setup.

Do I need to enable JavaScript?

Yes and no. The chord reference pages are HTML/CSS pages that work in any browser, load quickly and print cleanly without JavaScript. The interactive app uses JavaScript for sound and interaction.

What genres and progressions are included?

Blues, Jazz, Country, Rock & Roll, Folk and Pop: 21 progressions in all, from the 12-bar blues and the jazz ii–V–I to the '50s progression, the Canon, the Andalusian cadence and the pop I–V–vi–IV.

Does it work on my phone?

Yes. Warbling Wren is mobile ready. The piano shows fewer octaves with an octave shift, and the fretboard shows a 12-fret window that slides up the neck.

Can I turn the sound off?

Yes. The piano and the guitar each have a mute button for silent musical exploration.
