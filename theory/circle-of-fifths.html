The Circle of Fifths: The Map of Every Key

The circle of fifths is one picture that holds a surprising amount of music: every key, its key signature, its relative minor, the chords that belong to it, and how far it is from every other key. This page starts from zero and works up to the ideas professionals use. Read as far as you like.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. On the circle, all fourteen of these chords sit together in one small slice at the top.

How to read the diagrams: on the keyboard, the coloured keys are the chord's notes. On the guitar, each dot is a finger on a fret, an open circle above the nut is an open string, and × means don't play that string. The darkest dot is the chord's root, the note it's named after. A ✦ tag under a diagram marks a quirky shape: a guitar shape on three strings, up the neck or without its 5th, or a keyboard drawing inverted to fit one octave. Quirky isn't wrong. Like the knots in wood, these voicings have a character of their own.

Part 1: The basics

Twelve notes, twelve keys

Western music uses 12 different notes: the 7 white keys of a piano (C D E F G A B) and the 5 black keys between them (C♯/D♭, D♯/E♭, F♯/G♭, G♯/A♭, A♯/B♭). Each black key has two names: C♯ is the note just above C, and also the note just below D. After B the pattern starts again on the next C. Any of these 12 notes can be the home note of a song, so there are 12 major keys and 12 minor keys.

The circle of fifths puts the 12 keys in a ring, like the numbers on a clock. C is at the top, at 12 o'clock.

Why "fifths"?

A fifth is the distance from one note up to the note five letter names higher, counting the first note as 1. From C: C (1), D (2), E (3), F (4), G (5). So G is a fifth above C.

Going clockwise round the circle, each key is a fifth above the one before: C → G → D → A → E → B → F♯ → D♭ → A♭ → E♭ → B♭ → F, and one more fifth brings you back to C. Going counterclockwise, each key is a fifth below: C → F → B♭ → E♭ and so on.

Sharps on the right, flats on the left

Every major key except C needs some black keys. Those notes are shown at the start of the music as sharps (♯, one key higher) or flats (♭, one key lower), called the key signature. The circle shows them at a glance:

  • C at the top has none: all white keys.
  • Each step clockwise adds one sharp. G has 1, D has 2, A has 3, and so on.
  • Each step counterclockwise adds one flat. F has 1, B♭ has 2, E♭ has 3, and so on.
  • At the bottom the two sides meet. F♯ major (6 sharps) and G♭ major (6 flats) are the same notes on the piano, just written two ways. Notes like these, one key with two names, are called enharmonic.

The inner ring: relative minors

Every major key has a relative minor: a minor key that uses exactly the same notes and the same key signature, but treats a different note as home. C major and A minor both use only the white keys. C major feels at home on C and sounds bright; A minor feels at home on A and sounds darker. The two charts at the top of this page are exactly that pair.

The circle shows each relative minor just inside its major key: Am inside C, Em inside G, Dm inside F, and so on all the way round.

The chords of a key sit next to each other

Here's the trick that makes the circle so useful for playing. Find your key, then look at the keys on either side of it. In C major, those are F, C and G: the IV, I and V chords, the three most important chords in the key. Now look at the minor chords just inside them: Dm, Am and Em, the ii, vi and iii chords.

Those six neighbours are the six main chords of the key, and they all sound good together. It works in every key: turn the circle and the same shape picks out the chords. Thousands of songs use nothing else.

The order of sharps and flats

Sharps are always added in the same order: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯ E♯ B♯. That order is the circle itself, read clockwise from F. A common memory aid: Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle.

Flats come in the reverse order: B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭ C♭ F♭, the circle read counterclockwise from B. Memory aid: Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father.

Naming a key from its signature

  • Sharp keys: go up a half step (one key on the piano, one fret on the guitar) from the last sharp. The last sharp of 3 sharps is G♯, and a half step up is A, so it's A major (or its relative, F♯ minor).
  • Flat keys: the second-to-last flat is the key. With B♭ E♭ A♭ the second-to-last flat is E♭, so it's E♭ major (or C minor). One flat on its own is F major (or D minor).
  • Finding the relative minor: go down three half steps from the major key's home note (C → B → B♭ → A). Or count up to the 6th note of the major scale, which is why the minor chord is called vi.

Neighbours share almost everything

Keys next to each other on the circle differ by just one note. C major (C D E F G A B) and G major (G A B C D E F♯) share six of their seven notes; the only change is F to F♯. Every step further away shares one note fewer, down to just two shared notes at the far side of the circle.

That's why songs that change key (modulate) usually move to a neighbouring key: the move sounds natural, because most of the notes stay the same. A jump across the circle sounds dramatic.

Meet the chord: D7

D7 (D–F♯–A–C) comes from G major, one step clockwise of C. Its F♯ is the one note that separates the two keys, so playing it is the quickest way to tell your ear "we're in G now".

Scales are slices of the circle

Take any seven keys in a row on the outer ring and you have the notes of a major scale. F C G D A E B, seven neighbours, are exactly the notes of C major. Take five in a row, C G D A E, and you have the C major pentatonic scale, the five-note scale behind countless guitar solos and folk melodies.

Progressions as paths around the circle

When one chord's root is a fifth above the next chord's root, the change is one step counterclockwise on the circle. It's the strongest, most "arriving" chord change there is. Watch the path of these progressions:

  • ii–V–I (Dm – G – C): two steps counterclockwise, straight home. The backbone of jazz.
  • iii–vi–ii–V–I (Em – Am – Dm – G – C): four steps counterclockwise in a row, one at a time, all the way home.
  • I–IV–V (C – F – G): home and its two neighbours. That's why three-chord songs work in any key.
  • I–♭VII–IV (C – B♭ – F, then back to C): jumps two steps counterclockwise to B♭, then steps clockwise through F back to C, the jazz path walked in reverse. That backwards motion is part of the open, unresolved rock sound.

The chord that points at a chord

Every chord's "V chord", the one that pulls hardest toward it, is its clockwise neighbour. G pulls to C. D pulls to G. To steer a song toward any chord, play the chord one step clockwise of it first. In C, playing D7 before G is called a secondary dominant, and the circle shows you where to find it.

Meet the chord: A7

A7 (A–C♯–E–G) is the V7 chord of D minor, which makes it the secondary dominant of Dm. Its C♯ comes from D harmonic minor (or D major), not from C major. On the circle, A is one step clockwise of D.

Major and minor with the same home note

C major and C minor share a home note but not a key signature. C minor has three flats (B♭ E♭ A♭), and sits three steps counterclockwise of C major. Every minor key sits three steps counterclockwise of the major key with the same name. Keys like these, same home note but different scale, are called parallel keys.

Meet the chords: Cm and Fm

Both come from C minor (C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭). Cm is its home chord (C–E♭–G); Fm is its iv chord (F–A♭–C). Neither fits C major: Cm contains E♭ and Fm contains A♭.

Transposing

To move a song to a new key (transpose it), turn the circle. The chords keep the same positions relative to the key. A song in G using G, C, D (I, IV, V) moves to A as A, D, E: turn the circle two steps clockwise and every chord moves with it.

Why guitarists and horn players live on opposite sides

A guitar's open strings, E A D G, are four neighbours in a row on the circle, so the keys of E, A, D and G let open strings ring out. That's why so much guitar music sits on the sharp side. Trumpets, saxophones and clarinets are built in B♭ and E♭, so horn sections, brass bands and much big-band jazz sit on the flat side.

Part 3: For the professional

This part uses the language players, teachers and theorists use when they talk about the circle. Every term is explained as it comes up.

Why it closes: tuning and the Pythagorean comma

Every note is a vibration, and a pure fifth vibrates exactly 3 times for every 2 vibrations of the lower note (a frequency ratio of 3:2). Pitch distances are measured in cents: 100 cents to a half step, 1200 to an octave. Stack twelve pure fifths and you land about a quarter of a half step (roughly 23.5 cents) above the starting note seven octaves higher. That gap is the Pythagorean comma (531441:524288). In pure tuning the "circle" is really a spiral that never closes, and G♯ and A♭ are genuinely different pitches.

Twelve-tone equal temperament, the tuning of almost every modern piano and guitar, closes it by narrowing every fifth to exactly 700 cents, about 2 cents narrower than pure. Only then is F♯ the same note as G♭, and every key equally in tune. The circle as we use it, with enharmonic keys at the bottom, depends on that compromise.

A little history

The first known circle-of-fifths diagram appears in the Grammatika (1677) of the composer and theorist Nikolai Diletsky (Mykola Dyletsky), a guide to composition. Johann David Heinichen arrived at his own circle independently in Neu erfundene und gründliche Anweisung (1711) and published it again in Der General-Bass in der Composition (1728). His version was a single ring that placed each relative minor key right next to its major key. In 1737 David Kellner proposed the layout used today: major keys on an outer ring and their relative minors on an inner ring. Meantone and then equal temperament made modulating round the whole circle sound in tune.

The mathematics

A pitch class is a note name regardless of octave: every C on the piano is pitch class C. Number them C = 0, C♯ = 1, … B = 11. The circle of fifths is the chromatic circle (all 12 notes in half steps) multiplied by 7, counting round a 12-hour clock (mod 12): 0, 7, 2, 9, 4, 11, 6, 1, 8, 3, 10, 5. Because 7 shares no factor with 12, stepping by fifths visits all twelve pitch classes before it repeats. Only four intervals can do that: the half step, the fourth, the fifth and the major seventh.

The diatonic scale (any major scale or its modes) is seven consecutive positions on that circle, which is why it's "maximally even": its notes are spread as evenly as seven notes from twelve can be. Many of its properties follow from that: exactly one tritone (the B – F pair in C), every interval occurring a different number of times, and modulation to a neighbour changes exactly one note.

Key distance and closely related keys

The number of steps between two keys measures how far apart they sound. The closely related keys of a key are its relative, the keys one step either side, and their relatives. For C major: A minor, G major, E minor, F major and D minor. Those are exactly the keys of the chords vi, V, iii, IV and ii, the six-neighbour shape from Part 1. Classical modulation plans move mostly within that group, and a distant modulation is defined by how far round the circle it travels.

Harmonic motion around the circle

Fifth-descent chains

Root motion down a fifth is one counterclockwise step. Chains of these, often with every chord turned into a dominant 7th (a secondary dominant of the next), drive Baroque sequences, ragtime and jazz standards alike: III7 – VI7 – II7 – V7 – I.

Meet the chord: E7

E7 (E–G♯–B–D) is the V7 chord of A minor: its G♯ comes from A harmonic minor. In this chain it points at A7, which points at D7, and so on round the circle. Together with A7, D7 and G7 (all met above), it completes the chain.

Plagal and retrograde motion

Clockwise root motion (IV → I, ♭VII → IV → I) is called plagal when it ends on the tonic, and retrograde (backwards) when it runs against the usual counterclockwise flow. It's weaker in classical terms but central to rock, gospel and modal writing.

The tritone axis

Each key's opposite on the circle is a tritone away (three whole steps, half an octave). Dominant chords a tritone apart share their own tritone (G7 and D♭7 both contain B and F, with B spelled C♭ in D♭7), which is the basis of tritone substitution. In circle terms, a dominant is replaced by the chord directly across the circle.

Meet the chord: D♭7

D♭7 (D♭–F–A♭–C♭) is the V7 chord of G♭ major, directly across the circle from G. It resolves to C by sliding its root down a half step.

Dividing the circle

Moving home by a major third (four half steps) steps four positions at a time round the circle, splitting it into three equal parts. John Coltrane's "Giant Steps" moves between the keys of B, G and E♭ this way, linking them with V–I motion. Minor-third moves (three positions) split it into four parts, the same four notes that make up a diminished seventh chord.

Meet the chords: E and A♭

Neither fits C major. E (E–G♯–B) comes from E major, four steps clockwise of C; A♭ (A♭–C–E♭) comes from A♭ major, four steps counterclockwise. C, E and A♭ split the circle into three equal parts.

Beyond the circle

The circle shows one relationship, the fifth. Leonhard Euler's Tonnetz ("tone network", 1739) adds major and minor thirds on a second and third axis, forming a grid where each major or minor chord is a triangle. Later neo-Riemannian theory uses that grid to explain chromatic harmony, such as chromatic-mediant moves (C → E, C → A♭), that are distant on the circle but share common notes. The circle is the first map. The Tonnetz is the terrain.

Using it at the instrument

  • Practise anything (scales, ii–V–Is, voicings) around the circle counterclockwise. You'll cover all 12 keys in the order harmony most often travels, with only one note changing at each step.
  • On piano, play the C major scale, then G major, changing only the F to F♯. Keep going clockwise, adding one sharp each time, until you reach F♯.
  • On guitar, the circle is under your fingers: the 5th-string note at any fret is a fifth below the 6th-string note at the same fret. Move a root-6 barre chord to the 5th string at the same fret and you've stepped one place counterclockwise.
  • Find the six chords of any key without thinking: point at the key on the circle, take the neighbour on each side, then take the three minor chords just inside them.
  • When reharmonising, find the target chord, then approach it from its clockwise neighbour (a secondary dominant) or from directly across the circle (a tritone substitution).
  • When choosing a key to modulate to, count steps: one for a smooth change, three or more for a dramatic one.
  • Transpose by turning, not by recalculating: write a song's chords as numerals once, then read them off the circle in any key.
  • Play a progression in C Lydian, C Mixolydian and C Dorian back to back. You'll hear the circle's bright-to-dark order in one sitting.
  • In Warbling Wren, pick any chord and the circle shows its key, its relative, and the path of the progression you choose, in all 12 keys. Play the same progression from neighbouring chords and watch the whole shape turn.
