The Circle of Fifths: The Map of Every Key

The circle of fifths is one picture that holds a surprising amount of music: every key, its key signature, its relative minor, the chords that belong to it, and how far it is from every other key. This page starts from zero and works up to the ideas professionals use. Read as far as you like.

Part 1: The basics

Twelve notes, twelve keys

Western music uses 12 different notes: the 7 white keys of a piano (C D E F G A B) and the 5 black keys between them (C♯/D♭, D♯/E♭, F♯/G♭, G♯/A♭, A♯/B♭). After B the pattern starts again on the next C. Any of these 12 notes can be the home note of a song, so there are 12 major keys and 12 minor keys.

The circle of fifths puts the 12 keys in a ring, like the numbers on a clock. C is at the top, at 12 o'clock.

Why "fifths"?

A fifth is the distance from one note up to the note five letter names higher, counting the first note as 1. From C: C (1), D (2), E (3), F (4), G (5). So G is a fifth above C.

Going clockwise round the circle, each key is a fifth above the one before: C → G → D → A → E → B → F♯ → D♭ → A♭ → E♭ → B♭ → F, and one more fifth brings you back to C. Going counterclockwise, each key is a fifth below: C → F → B♭ → E♭ and so on.

Sharps on the right, flats on the left

Every major key except C needs some black keys. Those notes are shown at the start of the music as sharps (♯) or flats (♭), called the key signature. The circle shows them at a glance:

  • C at the top has none: all white keys.
  • Each step clockwise adds one sharp. G has 1, D has 2, A has 3, and so on.
  • Each step counterclockwise adds one flat. F has 1, B♭ has 2, E♭ has 3, and so on.
  • At the bottom the two sides meet. F♯ major (6 sharps) and G♭ major (6 flats) are the same notes on the piano, just written two ways.

The inner ring: relative minors

Every major key has a relative minor: a minor key that uses exactly the same notes and the same key signature, but treats a different note as home. C major and A minor both use only the white keys. C major feels at home on C and sounds bright; A minor feels at home on A and sounds darker.

The circle shows each relative minor just inside its major key: Am inside C, Em inside G, Dm inside F, and so on all the way round.

The chords of a key sit next to each other

Here's the trick that makes the circle so useful for playing. Find your key, then look at the keys on either side of it. In C major, those are F, C and G: the I, IV and V chords, the three most important chords in the key. Now look at the minor chords just inside them: Dm, Am and Em, the ii, vi and iii chords.

Those six neighbours are the six main chords of the key, and they all sound good together. It works in every key: turn the circle and the same shape picks out the chords. Thousands of songs use nothing else.

On WarblingWren, the circle has three rings: major keys on the outside, minor in the middle, and the key's diminished chords in the centre. The circle lights up the chord you choose and its relative, so you can see this shape in every key.

The order of sharps and flats

Sharps are always added in the same order: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯ E♯ B♯. That order is the circle itself, read clockwise from F. A common memory aid: Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle.

Flats come in the reverse order: B♭ E♭ A♭ D♭ G♭ C♭ F♭, the circle read counterclockwise from B. Memory aid: Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father.

Naming a key from its signature

  • Sharp keys: go up a half step from the last sharp. The last sharp of 3 sharps is G♯, and a half step up is A, so it's A major (or its relative, F♯ minor).
  • Flat keys: the second-to-last flat is the key. With B♭ E♭ A♭ the second-to-last flat is E♭, so it's E♭ major (or C minor). One flat on its own is F major (or D minor).
  • Finding the relative minor: go down three half steps from the major key's home note (C → B → B♭ → A). Or count up to the 6th note of the major scale, which is why the minor chord is called vi.

Neighbours share almost everything

Keys next to each other on the circle differ by just one note. C major (C D E F G A B) and G major (G A B C D E F♯) share six of their seven notes; the only change is F to F♯. Every step further away shares one note fewer, down to just two shared notes at the far side of the circle.

That's why songs that change key (modulate) usually move to a neighbouring key: the move sounds natural, because most of the notes stay the same. A jump across the circle sounds dramatic.

Scales are slices of the circle

Take any seven keys in a row on the outer ring and you have the notes of a major scale. F C G D A E B, seven neighbours, are exactly the notes of C major. Take five in a row, C G D A E, and you have the C major pentatonic scale, the five-note scale behind countless guitar solos and folk melodies.

Progressions as paths around the circle

When one chord's root is a fifth above the next chord's root, the change is one step counterclockwise on the circle. It's the strongest, most "arriving" chord change there is. Watch the path of these progressions:

  • ii–V–I (Dm – G – C): two steps counterclockwise, straight home. The backbone of jazz.
  • iii–vi–ii–V–I (Em – Am – Dm – G – C): four steps counterclockwise in a row, one at a time, all the way home.
  • I–IV–V (C – F – G): home and its two neighbours. That's why three-chord songs work in any key.
  • I–♭VII–IV (C – B♭ – F, then back to C): jumps two steps counterclockwise to B♭, then steps clockwise through F back to C, the jazz path walked in reverse. That backwards motion is part of the open, unresolved rock sound.

The chord that points at a chord

Every chord's "V chord", the one that pulls hardest toward it, is its clockwise neighbour. G pulls to C. D pulls to G. To steer a song toward any chord, play the chord one step clockwise of it first. In C, playing D7 before G is called a secondary dominant, and the circle shows you where to find it.

Major and minor with the same home note

C major and C minor share a home note but not a key signature. C minor has three flats, and sits three steps counterclockwise of C major. Every minor key sits three steps counterclockwise of the major key with the same name.

Brighter and darker: the modes

Transposing

To move a song to a new key, turn the circle. The chords keep the same positions relative to the key. A song in G using G, C and D (I, IV, V) moves to A as A, D and E: turn the circle two steps clockwise and every chord moves with it.

Why guitarists and horn players live on opposite sides

A guitar's open strings, E A D G, are four neighbours in a row on the circle, so the keys of E, A, D and G let open strings ring out. That's why so much guitar music sits on the sharp side. Trumpets, saxophones and clarinets are built in B♭ and E♭, so horn sections, brass bands and much big-band jazz sit on the flat side.

Part 3: For the professional

Why it closes: tuning and the Pythagorean comma

A pure fifth has a frequency ratio of 3:2. Stack twelve pure fifths and you land about a quarter of a semitone (roughly 23.5 cents) above the starting note seven octaves higher. That gap is the Pythagorean comma (531441:524288). In pure tuning the "circle" is really a spiral that never closes, and G♯ and A♭ are genuinely different pitches.

Twelve-tone equal temperament closes it by narrowing every fifth to exactly 700 cents, about 2 cents narrower than pure. Only then is F♯ the same note as G♭, and every key equally in tune. The circle as we use it, with enharmonic keys at the bottom, depends on that compromise.

A little history

Circular arrangements of keys appeared in the late 17th century, in Nikolai Diletsky's writings on composition. Johann David Heinichen published a circle of major and relative minor keys in the early 18th century as a practical guide to modulation. The modern two-ring diagram grew out of that tradition, and the rise of equal temperament made it a true circle.

The mathematics

Number the pitch classes C = 0, C♯ = 1, … B = 11. The circle of fifths is the chromatic circle multiplied by 7 (mod 12): 0, 7, 2, 9, 4, 11, 6, 1, 8, 3, 10, 5. Because 7 shares no factor with 12, stepping by fifths visits all twelve pitch classes before it repeats. Only four intervals can do that: the semitone, the fourth, the fifth and the major seventh.

The diatonic scale is seven consecutive positions on that circle, which is why it's "maximally even": its notes are spread as evenly as seven notes from twelve can be. Many of its properties follow from that: exactly one tritone, every interval occurring a different number of times, and modulation to a neighbour changes exactly one note.

Key distance and closely related keys

The number of steps between two keys measures how far apart they sound. The closely related keys of a key are its relative, the keys one step either side, and their relatives. For C major: A minor, G major, E minor, F major and D minor. Those are exactly the keys of the diatonic chords ii, iii, IV, V and vi, the six-neighbour shape from Part 1. Classical modulation plans move mostly within that group, and a distant modulation is defined by how far round the circle it travels.

Harmonic motion around the circle

  • Fifth-descent chains. Root motion down a fifth is one counterclockwise step. Chains of these, often with secondary dominants (III7 – VI7 – II7 – V7 – I), drive Baroque sequences, ragtime and jazz standards alike.
  • Plagal and retrograde motion. Clockwise root motion (IV → I, ♭VII → IV → I) is weaker in classical terms but central to rock, gospel and modal writing.
  • The tritone axis. Each key's opposite on the circle is a tritone away. Dominant chords a tritone apart share their own tritone (G7 and D♭7 both contain B/C♭ and F), which is the basis of tritone substitution. In circle terms, a dominant is replaced by the chord directly across the circle.
  • Dividing the circle. Moving tonics by major thirds steps four positions at a time, splitting the circle into three equal parts. John Coltrane's "Giant Steps" moves between B, G and E♭ this way, linking them with V–I motion. Minor-third moves (three positions) split it into four parts, the axis of the diminished seventh chord.

Beyond the circle

The circle shows one relationship, the fifth. Leonhard Euler's Tonnetz (1739) adds major and minor thirds on a second and third axis, forming a grid where triads are triangles. Later neo-Riemannian theory uses that grid to explain chromatic harmony, such as chromatic-mediant moves (C → E, C → A♭), that are distant on the circle but share common tones. The circle is the first map. The Tonnetz is the terrain.

Using it at the instrument

  • Practise anything (scales, ii–V–Is, voicings) around the circle counterclockwise. You'll cover all 12 keys in the order harmony most often travels, with only one note changing at each step.
  • When reharmonising, find the target chord, then approach it from its clockwise neighbour (a secondary dominant) or from directly across the circle (a tritone substitution).
  • When choosing a key to modulate to, count steps: one for a smooth change, three or more for a dramatic one.
