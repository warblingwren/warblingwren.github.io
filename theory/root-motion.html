Root Motion: How Chords Move from One to the Next

Why does one chord change sound strong and final, another smooth and gentle, and another bold and surprising? A big part of the answer is root motion: how far the bottom note of each chord moves when the chords change. This page starts from zero and works up to the ideas professionals use. Read as far as you like.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. Every root movement on this page travels between them.

Part 1: The basics

What is a chord's root?

Every chord is built up from one note, its root. The chord is named after it. The C chord is built on C (C–E–G), the F chord on F (F–A–C), and the G chord on G (G–B–D). In the guitar diagrams on this page, the root is the darkest dot.

Root motion is simply how far you travel from one chord's root to the next one's. Going from C to F, the root moves from C up to F. Going from F to G, it moves from F up to G.

Measuring the distance

Musicians measure the distance between two notes, called an interval, by counting letter names, and they count the starting note as 1. From C to F: C (1), D (2), E (3), F (4). So C up to F is a fourth. From C to G: C → D → E → F → G is five letters, a fifth. From C to D is a second (also called a step). From C to E is a third.

Up a fourth is the same as down a fifth

From C you can reach F by going up four letters (C → D → E → F) or down five letters (C → B → A → G → F). You land on the same note name, just in a different octave (a higher or lower copy of the same note). Because a chord change sounds like the same change whichever way the bass player goes, musicians treat these as one move. The pairs always add up to 9:

Going up a…is the same note as going down a…Example from C
secondseventhC → D
thirdsixthC → E
fourthfifthC → F
fifthfourthC → G

That leaves just three kinds of root motion to learn: by a fifth, by a third and by a step. Each one has its own sound.

The three sounds

  • By a fifth: strong. C to F, or G to C. This is the sound of a song moving forward with purpose, or coming home. It's the most common root motion in all of music.
  • By a third: gentle. C to Am, or Am to F. The change is so smooth you can almost miss it, like a change of lighting rather than a change of scene.
  • By a step: bold. F to G, or G to Am. Next-door neighbours, yet they sound completely different, so the change gives a strong push or lift.

Part 2: Going deeper

Why they sound different: shared notes

The sound of a chord change depends a lot on how many notes the two chords have in common. Notes the chords share stay put, and only the rest move. Using chords built from the C major scale:

  • A fifth apart: one shared note. C (C–E–G) and F (F–A–C) share only C. One note anchors the change, and the others move, so it feels like real progress with a thread holding it together.
  • A third apart: two shared notes. C (C–E–G) and Am (A–C–E) share C and E. Only one note changes, from G to A. That's why the move feels so soft.
  • A step apart: no shared notes. F (F–A–C) and G (G–B–D) have nothing in common. Every note moves, so the change sounds fresh and energetic.

A tritone is the distance of three whole steps, exactly half an octave: C to F♯. F♯ isn't in the C major scale, which is one reason a tritone root move sounds so far from home.

Fifths and the circle of fifths

The circle of fifths puts all 12 notes in a ring where each neighbour is a fifth away. Going clockwise, each step goes up a fifth: C, G, D, A… Going counterclockwise, each step goes down a fifth: C, F, B♭, E♭…

So root motion by a fifth is simply one step around the circle. Counterclockwise steps (down a fifth) feel like falling toward home. Clockwise steps (up a fifth) feel like heading out. That's why V → I sounds like the end of a sentence: G is the clockwise neighbour of C, and stepping back from G to C is a fall toward home. The circle in Warbling Wren lets you watch a progression's path around the ring.

Part 3: For the professional

This part uses the language players and teachers use when they talk about root motion. Every term is explained as it comes up.

Fundamental bass

Root motion as a way of explaining harmony goes back to Jean-Philippe Rameau's Traité de l'harmonie (Treatise on Harmony, 1722). Rameau described the basse fondamentale (fundamental bass): an imaginary line made only of chord roots, which may never actually be played. He argued that motion by a fifth is the strongest and most natural. Roman-numeral analysis still rests on that idea: the numerals track roots, not whatever note the bass happens to play.

Root versus bass

The root and the lowest note aren't always the same. A slash chord names the chord, then the bass note after the slash: C/E is a C chord with E in the bass, and its root is still C. Playing a chord with a note other than the root in the bass is called an inversion.

That means a progression can move by fifths in its roots while the bass line moves by step. You get the strength of fifth motion with the smoothness of a walking bass.

Meet the chords: G/B and Em/G

Both are chords of the C major scale with a different note in the bass. G/B is G with its 3rd, B, at the bottom. Em/G is Em with its 3rd, G, at the bottom. The roots stay G and E; only the lowest note changes.

A pedal point does the opposite: the bass holds one note while the roots above it move. Holding C under C → F → G → C keeps the music anchored at home even as the chords change.

Strong, weak and superstrong

Arnold Schoenberg's book Structural Functions of Harmony sorts root motion by what happens to the old root:

  • Strong (Schoenberg called it "ascending"): down a fifth or down a third. The old root becomes a lesser note of the new chord (C becomes the 5th of F, or the 3rd of Am), so the new chord seems to take the old one over.
  • Weak ("descending"): up a fifth or up a third. The new root was already a lesser note of the old chord (G was the 5th of C, E its 3rd), so the change can sound like a step backwards. Schoenberg advised using these with care.
  • Superstrong: by a step. No shared notes, so the new chord is entirely new material. It's especially common going into a cadence (the chord change that ends a phrase), as in IV → V.

These categories describe tendencies, not rules. The popularity of up-a-fifth moves in rock (♭VII → IV → I, or IV → I) shows that a "weak" motion can be exactly the sound a style is after.

Voice leading

Voice leading is how each individual note of one chord moves to a note of the next. Root motion predicts it:

  • With fifths, hold the one shared note and move the other two by step.
  • With thirds, hold two and move one.
  • With steps, there's nothing to hold. Move the upper notes in the opposite direction to the bass (contrary motion), so no two parts move in parallel fifths or octaves, which makes the parts blur into one.

Seventh chords change the counts. In ii7 → V7 → Imaj7, each chord's 7th steps down to the next chord's 3rd. That's why falling-fifth chains of seventh chords voice so smoothly.

Chromatic root motion

Chromatic means using notes from outside the key. Three techniques use chromatic roots on purpose.

Tritone substitution

Replace G7 with D♭7. Both contain the same tritone: B and F (B is spelled C♭ in D♭7). The resolution to C still works, but the root now falls a half step (D♭ → C) instead of a fifth. ii – ♭II7 – I turns a falling-fifths progression into a chromatic bass line that slides down by half steps.

Meet the chord: D♭7

D♭7 (D♭–F–A♭–C♭) is the V7 chord of G♭ major, a key far from C. It shares its two most restless notes with G7, which is why it can take G7's place. Its root sits exactly a tritone away from G, directly opposite on the circle of fifths.

Chromatic mediants

A mediant is a chord a third away. A chromatic mediant is a chord a third away with the same quality (major to major) that isn't in the same key: C → E, C → A♭, C → E♭. These share one note instead of two, which gives the bright, colour-shifting sound heard in film scores.

Meet the chords: E, A♭ and E♭

None of these fits the C major scale. E (E–G♯–B) comes from E major; A♭ (A♭–C–E♭) and E♭ (E♭–G–B♭) both come from C minor (C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭). Each shares exactly one note with C. E keeps its E, A♭ keeps its C, and E♭ keeps its G.

Sequences

A sequence is a repeating root-motion pattern moved step by step through the key, such as the descending-fifths sequence (vi–ii–V–I or iii–vi–ii–V–I) or the Canon's down-a-fourth, up-a-step pattern. A sequence creates forward motion on its own, which is why so many progressions are built on one.

Putting it to work

When writing or reharmonising (choosing new chords for an existing melody), think in root motion first, then pick chord qualities:

  • Fifths drive toward a goal.
  • Thirds stretch the time spent around a function without changing it (I → vi keeps you home).
  • Steps add contrast and push into cadences.
  • Chromatic motion adds colour without losing direction.

Using it at the instrument

  • Play only the roots. Before playing any progression, play just its root notes with one finger or on the low strings, and name each move: fifth, third or step.
  • On piano, keep shared notes still. Going from C to Am, leave C and E where they are and move only G up to A. Going from C to F, hold C and move the other two fingers.
  • On guitar, find the fifth below. The root on the 5th string is a fifth below the root on the 6th string at the same fret (G at fret 3 on the 6th string, C at fret 3 on the 5th). Moving a barre chord from the 6th string to the 5th string at the same fret is a fall of a fifth.
  • Walk the circle. Play C7 → F7 → B♭7 → E♭7 and keep going counterclockwise around the circle of fifths. You'll hear why jazz players practise every tune this way.
  • Swap a fifth for a step. In I–IV–V–I, replace G with Dm and listen to how the arrival gets softer (step, then third) instead of firm (fifth).
  • Try the walking-bass version of a song you know: wherever the roots move by a fifth, put a chord in its first inversion (3rd in the bass) so the bass line moves by step instead.
  • Hold a pedal. Keep C ringing in the bass (low string or left hand) under C → F → G → C and hear the chords move while home stays fixed.
  • Replace G7 with D♭7 at the end of a phrase and listen to the bass slide down a half step into C.
  • In Warbling Wren, watch the circle while a progression plays. Short hops between neighbours are fifths; anything else is a third, a step or a chromatic move.
