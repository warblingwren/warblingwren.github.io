Root Motion: How Chords Move from One to the Next

Why does one chord change sound strong and final, another smooth and gentle, and another bold and surprising? A big part of the answer is root motion: how far the bottom note of each chord moves when the chords change. This page starts from zero and works up to the ideas professionals use. Read as far as you like.

Part 1: The basics

What is a chord's root?

Every chord is built up from one note, its root. The chord is named after it. The C chord is built on C (C–E–G), the F chord on F (F–A–C), and the G chord on G (G–B–D).

Root motion is simply how far you travel from one chord's root to the next one's. Going from C to F, the root moves from C up to F. Going from F to G, it moves from F up to G.

Measuring the distance

Musicians measure the distance between two notes by counting letter names, and they count the starting note as 1. From C to F: C (1), D (2), E (3), F (4). So C up to F is a fourth. From C to G: C, D, E, F, G is five letters, a fifth. From C to D is a second (also called a step). From C to E is a third.

Up a fourth is the same as down a fifth

From C you can reach F by going up four letters (C D E F) or down five letters (C B A G F). You land on the same note name, just in a different octave. Because a chord change sounds like the same change whichever way the bass player goes, musicians treat these as one move. The pairs always add up to 9:

secondseventhC → D

fifthfourthC → G

That leaves just three kinds of root motion to learn: by a fifth, by a third and by a step. Each one has its own sound.

The three sounds

  • By a fifth: strong. C to F, or G to C. This is the sound of a song moving forward with purpose, or coming home. It's the most common root motion in all of music.
  • By a third: gentle. C to Am, or Am to F. The change is so smooth you can almost miss it, like a change of lighting rather than a change of scene.
  • By a step: bold. F to G, or G to Am. Next-door neighbours, yet they sound completely different, so the change gives a strong push or lift.

Part 2: Going deeper

Why they sound different: shared notes

The sound of a chord change depends a lot on how many notes the two chords have in common. Notes the chords share stay put, and only the rest move. Using chords built from the C major scale:

  • A fifth apart: one shared note. C (C–E–G) and F (F–A–C) share only C. One note anchors the change, and the others move, so it feels like real progress with a thread holding it together.
  • A third apart: two shared notes. C (C–E–G) and Am (A–C–E) share C and E. Only one note changes, from G to A. That's why the move feels so soft.
  • A step apart: no shared notes. F (F–A–C) and G (G–B–D) have nothing in common. Every note moves, so the change sounds fresh and energetic.

Fifths and the circle of fifths

The circle of fifths puts all 12 notes in a ring where each neighbour is a fifth away. Going clockwise, each step goes up a fifth: C, G, D, A… Going counterclockwise, each step goes down a fifth: C, F, B♭, E♭…

So root motion by a fifth is simply one step around the circle. Counterclockwise steps (down a fifth) feel like falling toward home. Clockwise steps (up a fifth) feel like heading out. That's why V → I sounds like the end of a sentence: G is the clockwise neighbour of C, and stepping back from G to C is a fall toward home. The circle on WarblingWren lets you watch a progression's path around the ring.

Root motion in the progressions on this site

  • ii–V–I (Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7): down a fifth, then down a fifth again. Two falls in a row, which is why it's jazz's favourite way to arrive home.
  • iii–vi–ii–V (Em7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7, then C): four falling fifths in a row, E → A → D → G → C, one step at a time around the circle.
  • 12-bar blues (C7 – F7 – G7): C → F is down a fifth, F → C up a fifth, C → G up a fifth, G → F down a step, F → C up a fifth, C → G up a fifth again, and the last bar's G → C falls a fifth back to the top.
  • I–vi–IV–V (C – Am – F – G): down a third, down a third, up a step, then G → C falls a fifth to start again. Two gentle moves, one push, one arrival.
  • Canon (C – G – Am – Em – F): up a fifth, up a step, up a fifth, up a step. The same two-move pattern repeats, which is why it sounds so orderly.
  • Andalusian cadence (Am – G – F – E): down a step, down a step, down a step. A pure staircase, then E → Am falls a fifth home.
  • I–♭VII–IV (C – B♭ – F): down a step, then up a fifth, then F → C up a fifth. The falling-fifths path of jazz, walked backwards. That reversal is a big part of the open, unresolved rock sound.

Part 3: For the professional

Fundamental bass

Root motion as a way of explaining harmony goes back to Jean-Philippe Rameau's Traité de l'harmonie (1722). Rameau described the basse fondamentale, a theoretical line of chord roots that may never be played as written, and argued that motion by fifth is the strongest and most natural. Roman-numeral analysis still rests on that idea: it tracks roots, not the bass part.

Root versus bass

The root and the lowest note aren't always the same. C/E (a C chord with E in the bass) still has root C. A progression can move by fifths in its roots while the bass line moves by step through inversions, which gives the strength of fifth motion with the smoothness of a stepwise bass. Pedal points do the opposite: the bass stays put while the roots above it move.

Strong, weak and superstrong

Arnold Schoenberg's Structural Functions of Harmony sorts root motion by what happens to the old root:

  • Strong (ascending): down a fifth or down a third. The old root becomes a lesser note of the new chord (C becomes the 5th of F, or the 3rd of Am), so the new chord seems to take the old one over.
  • Weak (descending): up a fifth or up a third. The new root was already a lesser note of the old chord (G was the 5th of C, E its 3rd), so the change can sound like a step backwards. Schoenberg advised using these with care.
  • Superstrong: by a step. No shared notes, so the new chord is entirely new material. It's especially common going into a cadence, as in IV → V.

These categories describe tendencies, not rules. The popularity of up-a-fifth moves in rock (♭VII → IV → I, or IV → I) shows that a "weak" motion can be exactly the sound a style is after.

Voice leading

Root motion predicts voice leading. With fifths, hold the one shared note and move the other two by step. With thirds, hold two and move one. With steps, there's nothing to hold, so move the upper voices against the bass (contrary motion) to avoid parallel fifths and octaves. Seventh chords change the counts. In ii7 – V7 – Imaj7, for example, each chord's 7th steps down to the next chord's 3rd. That's why falling-fifth chains of seventh chords voice so smoothly.

Chromatic root motion

  • Tritone substitution. Replace G7 with D♭7: both contain the same tritone (B and F, spelled C♭ and F in D♭7). The resolution to C still works, but the root now falls a half step (D♭ → C) instead of a fifth. ii – ♭II7 – I turns a falling-fifths progression into a chromatic bass descent.
  • Chromatic mediants. Third motion between chords of the same quality that aren't in the same key: C → E, C → A♭, C → E♭. They share one note instead of two, which gives the bright, colour-shifting sound heard in film scores.
  • Sequences. A repeating root-motion pattern moved through the key, such as the descending-fifths sequence (vi – ii – V – I or iii – vi – ii – V – I) or the Canon's down-a-fourth, up-a-step pattern. A sequence creates forward motion on its own, which is why so many progressions are built on one.

Putting it to work

When writing or reharmonising, think in root motion first, then pick chord qualities:

  • Fifths drive toward a goal.
  • Thirds stretch the time spent around a function without changing it (I → vi keeps you home).
  • Steps add contrast and push into cadences.
  • Chromatic motion adds colour without losing direction.
