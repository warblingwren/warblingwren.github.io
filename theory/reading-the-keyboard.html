Reading the Keyboard

A piano has 88 keys, but it only has 12 different notes. The same pattern of 12 repeats from one end to the other. Once you know the pattern, you can find any note on any piano or keyboard. This page shows you how, then puts chords and arpeggios under your fingers.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). If you find these chords on your piano or guitar now, every example further down will already be under your fingers. Every chord here is played on the keyboard drawings, so you can copy them key for key.

How to read the diagrams: on the keyboard, the coloured keys are the chord's notes. On the guitar, each dot is a finger on a fret, an open circle above the nut is an open string, and × means don't play that string. The darkest dot is the chord's root, the note it's named after. A ✦ tag under a diagram marks a quirky shape: a guitar shape on three strings, up the neck or without its 5th, or a keyboard drawing inverted to fit one octave. Quirky isn't wrong. Like the knots in wood, these voicings have a character of their own.

Part 1: The basics

Twos and threes

If you look at the black keys, you'll find they come in little groups: two, then three, then two, then three, all the way along. That pattern is your map. The white key just to the left of any group of two black keys is always C.

Naming every key

The white keys are named with the letters A to G, then the letters start again. The black keys take their names from their neighbours: the black key just above C is C♯ ("C sharp", sharp meaning a half step higher), and it's also D♭ ("D flat", flat meaning a half step lower). One key, two names. Which name you use depends on the key of the music.

Octaves and middle C

From one C to the next is an octave. To say which C you mean, musicians number the octaves. The C nearest the middle of the piano is C4, called middle C. The one an octave lower is C3, the one an octave higher is C5, and so on.

Written music has two staves for piano, one for each hand, and middle C sits right between them, on its own short line.

Try it The same chord in three octaves

  • What you'll hear: C low, in the middle, then high.
  • Listen for: the same chord sounding darker low down and brighter up high. On this site, each octave has its own colour.
  • That's: octaves: the same notes, higher or lower.

Part 2: Going deeper

Chord shapes in the hand

A three-note chord (a triad) fits neatly under three fingers: thumb (1), middle finger (3) and little finger (5). The pattern under your fingers is play one key, skip one, play one, skip one, play one. In C major, three of the major chords are all white keys: C, F and G. Same shape, three places.

Arpeggio shapes in the hand

An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time instead of all at once. Pianists use arpeggios everywhere: flowing left-hand accompaniments, the rippling openings of countless ballads, and right-hand lines that outline the chords underneath them. Its notes are the chord's root, 3rd and 5th, repeated in higher octaves, and because they're the chord's own notes, they always sound right over that chord.

To play one smoothly across two octaves, your thumb tucks under your hand to start the next octave, the same movement as in a scale but across a wider gap. If you let the wrist travel sideways with the hand instead of twisting at each crossing, the thumb arrives in time without a bump. For the C arpeggio, the usual fingering is:

NoteCEGCEGC
Right hand1231235
Left hand5421421

Finger 1 is the thumb, 5 the little finger. In the right hand, the thumb passes under after G; in the left hand, finger 4 crosses over the thumb.

To read the drawing in the Try it below, follow the staff from left to right, one note per bar: the labels underneath are the right-hand fingers, and each note on the staff is the same colour as its key on the keyboard, so you can find every note by colour. The darkest notes are the root, C, so you can see where each octave of the arpeggio begins.

Try it A two-octave C arpeggio

  • What you'll hear: C, E, G up through two octaves, one note at a time, then back down.
  • Listen for: an even sound as your thumb passes under, with no accent or gap at the crossing. The numbers under the notes are the right-hand fingers.
  • That's: an arpeggio across the keyboard.

The left hand often plays a chord as a repeating broken pattern instead of a straight arpeggio. The most famous is the Alberti bass, named after the composer Domenico Alberti and heard all through Mozart and Haydn: lowest note, highest, middle, highest, over and over. It keeps the harmony moving under a melody without ever sounding the whole chord at once.

Playing an arpeggio straight up and down is only the start. These are ways to practise it that also turn it into music:

  • Start from the 3rd or the 5th instead of the root, so the arpeggio begins on a different colour of the same chord.
  • Play it in a pattern, such as root, 3rd, 5th, 3rd, then the same from the next note up, so the fingers learn to move in both directions.
  • Pick a progression such as C → Am → F → G and play each chord's arpeggio, changing when the chord changes. The notes that move are the ones that make each chord sound new.
  • Play the arpeggio slowly with a metronome, one note per click, then two, then three in a triplet feel.
  • Turn a progression into an accompaniment: the left hand plays an Alberti bass or a slow arpeggio while the right hand plays a melody or simple chords on top.
  • When you improvise a melody, land on an arpeggio note, especially the root or the 3rd, whenever the chord changes. That one habit makes improvised lines sound connected to the harmony.

Part 3: For the professional

These details matter once you start reading scores, writing parts, or using the colour system on this site to read voicings at a glance.

The full range: 88 keys

A full piano runs from A0, the lowest A, to C8, the highest C: seven full octaves plus a few keys. Octave numbers change at C, not at A, so the key just below C4 is B3. The Warbling Wren app shows the full 88-key range.

The octave colours in Warbling Wren

On this site, every octave has its own colour family, and inside each chord the shade shows the note's job: the root darkest, then the 3rd, the 5th and the 7th. So a chord voiced across two octaves shows two colour families, and you can see at a glance which notes are low and which are high. The same colours appear on the guitar neck, on the staves and in the note lines.

Try it Reading a spread-out chord by colour

  • What you'll hear: C close together, then spread across two octaves.
  • Listen for: a fuller, wider sound from the same three note names.
  • That's: an open voicing: the colours show which octave each note is in.

Using it at the instrument

  • Find every C on the piano by its two black keys, then every F by its three. Do it with your eyes closed by touch.
  • Say the note names as you play up the white keys. Then play the black keys and say both names.
  • Play one chord in every octave, from the bottom of the piano to the top, and listen to how its character changes.
  • Use the 1-3-5 hand shape for every triad, and let the black keys tell you which chords are major or minor.
  • Practise arpeggios slowly with a steady beat, thumb passing smoothly under.
  • In Warbling Wren, pick an octave row on the piano and watch the colours change as the chord moves up and down.
