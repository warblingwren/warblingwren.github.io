How Chords Are Built

A chord is three or more notes played at the same time. Every chord on this site, from a simple C to a jazzy Bm7♭5, is built the same way: by stacking intervals on top of one note. This page shows you how, one layer at a time. If "interval" is a new word, the Intervals page explains it from scratch.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. Every chord on this page is built on these notes, until we deliberately reach outside them.

How to read the diagrams: on the keyboard, the coloured keys are the chord's notes. On the guitar, each dot is a finger on a fret, an open circle above the nut is an open string, and × means don't play that string. The darkest dot is the chord's root, the note it's named after. A ✦ tag under a diagram marks a quirky shape: a guitar shape on three strings, up the neck or without its 5th, or a keyboard drawing inverted to fit one octave. Quirky isn't wrong. Like the knots in wood, these voicings have a character of their own.

Part 1: The basics

Stacking thirds

Start with one note: C. This is the chord's root, the note it's named after. Now skip one letter and add the next: E. That's a third. Skip one more and add G: another third. Those three notes, C, E and G, are the C chord.

That's the whole recipe: root, skip a letter, add a note, skip a letter, add a note. The middle note is called the third (it's a third above the root) and the top note is the fifth. On a staff you can see the stack: the notes sit on three lines in a row, like rungs on a ladder.

The four triads

A three-note chord built this way is called a triad. Thirds come in two sizes, major (4 half steps) and minor (3 half steps), so there are four ways to stack two of them. Each gives a triad with its own sound:

  • Major = major third, then minor third: C (C–E–G). Bright and settled.
  • Minor = minor third, then major third: Am (A–C–E). Darker, more serious.
  • Diminished = minor third, then minor third: B° (B–D–F). Tight and tense.
  • Augmented = major third, then major third: C+ (C–E–G♯). Stretched and dreamy.

Meet the chord: C+

C+ (C augmented) is C with its top note raised a half step, to G♯. That note isn't in C major, so the chord comes from outside the key: it appears in A harmonic minor, a version of A minor with a raised seventh note.

How the colours show a chord

On this site, every chord's notes are coloured by their job. The root is darkest, then the 3rd, then the 5th, and the 7th (if there is one) is lightest. Look at the dots in the guitar diagrams and the keys on the keyboards: you can always spot the root, even in a chord you've never seen. The colour family also changes with the octave, so a high C and a low C look related but not identical.

Suspended chords

A suspended chord swaps the 3rd for a different note: the 4th (Csus4) or the 2nd (Csus2). With no 3rd, the chord is neither major nor minor. It sounds open, like a question, and often moves back to the plain chord.

The augmented 7th

Add a minor 7th to an augmented triad and you get the augmented 7th: Caug7 (C–E–G♯–B♭). It's a dominant 7th with its 5th stretched up a half step, and it pulls even harder toward its target.

Meet the chord: Caug7

Caug7 has two notes from outside C major: G♯ and B♭. It's usually found as a stronger version of the V chord in F major, where it leans into F.

Arpeggios: a chord one note at a time

You don't have to play a chord's notes together. Play them one after another, bottom to top, and you get an arpeggio (from the Italian word for harp). Arpeggios are how most melodies outline a chord, and how guitarists pick through a chord.

Part 3: For the professional

These are the details working musicians run into: different ways of writing the same chord, and chords with unusual shapes.

One chord, many names

Chord symbols aren't fully standard. The same chord can be written several ways, depending on the book or the player:

ChordAlso written
half-diminishedBm7♭5 = Bø7 = Bm7♭5
diminished 7thG♯°7 = G♯dim7
augmentedC+ = Caug = C(♯5)
major 7thCmaj7 = CΔ7 = CM7
minorAm = Amin = A−

This site writes them the way the badges show, so every page matches the app.

Chords that split the octave evenly

Two chords are perfectly symmetrical. The augmented triad stacks major thirds, 4 half steps each, so three of them make exactly one octave. The diminished 7th stacks minor thirds, 3 half steps each, so four make one octave. On the circle of fifths, the augmented triad is an even triangle and the diminished 7th is a perfect square.

Because they're symmetrical, these chords have no single obvious root. C+, E+ and A♭+ are the same three keys on a piano, just with different names. That makes them great "pivot" chords that can lead almost anywhere.

A chord's notes, not its name, decide its job

C6 and Am7 contain exactly the same four notes: C → E → G → A. Which one you hear depends on the lowest note and on the key around it. So what a chord does in a song (sound like home, move away, pull back) depends on its notes and their surroundings, not on its symbol. That's the subject of the Chord Functions page.

Using it at the instrument

  • Build every chord from its root. Find the root, count up a third, then another third. On piano, that's "play one, skip one, play one" with the white keys in C major.
  • Switch one note at a time. Play C, then lower the E to E♭ for Cm, then raise it to F for Csus4. You'll feel how one note changes the whole mood.
  • Add the 7th last. Learn a triad first, then add the 7th on top. On guitar, that usually means lifting one finger or adding one.
  • Say the chord as you play it: "root, third, fifth". Naming the notes builds the picture faster than memorising shapes.
  • Play arpeggios slowly, one note per beat. Then play the same notes together as a chord.
  • In Warbling Wren, pick a chord type and tap through all 12 roots: the colours show the same recipe on every root.
