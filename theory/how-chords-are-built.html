How Chords Are Built

A chord is three or more notes played at the same time. Every chord on this site, from a simple C to a jazzy Bm7♭5, is built the same way: start on one note, then stack more notes on top of it at set distances. Those distances are called intervals. If that word is new, the Intervals page explains it from scratch, and it's worth reading first. This page builds chords one layer at a time, so you can see exactly where every note comes from.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. Every chord on this page is built from these notes, until we deliberately reach outside them.

Part 1: The basics

Stacking thirds

Every chord starts with one note, called the root. It's the note the chord is named after: the C chord has C as its root. To build the rest of the chord, you add notes above the root, and the rule for which notes to add is simple: skip a letter each time.

Musical note names use the letters A to G, then start over. Start on C. Skip D and take the next letter, E. Skip F and take the next, G. That gives three notes, C, E and G, and together they make the C chord.

Why "thirds"? Count the letters from C to E, including both: C, D, E. That's three letters, so musicians call that distance a third. E to G is another third (E, F, G). So a chord built this way is "stacked in thirds", and each note gets a name for its place in the stack:

  • The root (C): the bottom of the stack, and the chord's name.
  • The third (E): a third above the root.
  • The fifth (G): a third above that, which is five letters above the root (C, D, E, F, G).

Seeing the stack on a staff

Written music, called a staff, is one of the clearest ways to see this. Here's how to read the drawing below. The five horizontal lines are the staff. Every line, and every space between two lines, is one letter name, and the higher a note sits, the higher it sounds. Left to right is time, and the vertical lines divide the music into bars. Notes stacked straight up and down in the same bar are played together, as a chord.

Now look at the three bars. Bar one has just the root. Bar two adds the third, which sits on the next line up. Bar three adds the fifth, on the line above that. Because each note skips one letter, the chord sits line, line, line, like rungs on a ladder. Every chord stacked in thirds looks like that on a staff.

Seeing the stack on a note line

The staff shows letter names, but it hides one thing: how far apart the notes really are on the instrument. For that we'll use a second picture, the note line. It shows exactly the same chord, so here's how to read it:

  • Each column is one piano key (or one guitar fret), in order from low to high. White columns are white keys; shaded columns are black keys, with both of their names at the top.
  • Each row is one step of the build, matching the three bars of the staff above: the root alone, then the third added, then the fifth.
  • Each coloured dot is a note in the chord, in the same colours the keyboards and guitar diagrams use. The darkest dot, with a ring, is the root.
  • The bracket above a row measures a distance in half steps: one half step is one column, the very next key.

That last point is the key to the next section. The staff says both gaps are thirds. The note line shows one is 4 half steps and the other is 3. Those two sizes have names: a 4-half-step third is a major third, and a 3-half-step third is a minor third.

The four triads

A chord with three notes, root, third and fifth, is called a triad ("tri" means three). Every triad is two thirds stacked on top of each other. Each third can be major (4 half steps) or minor (3 half steps), so there are exactly four ways to stack them, and each one makes a different kind of triad:

TriadBottom thirdTop thirdExampleHow it sounds
Majormajor (4)minor (3)C (C–E–G)bright, open, settled
Minorminor (3)major (4)Am (A–C–E)darker, softer, more serious

Notice something about major and minor: they use the same two sizes of third, just in the opposite order. And in both, the root and the fifth are the same distance apart, 7 half steps. The only note that changes between them is the middle one, the third. That single note is what makes a chord sound happy or sad.

Here are the four triads on a staff and on a note line. On the staff they all look alike, line, line, line, because all four are stacked in thirds. On the note line you can see the difference: look at the two brackets in each row.

Meet the chord: C+

C+ (C augmented) is C with its top note raised a half step, from G to G♯. G♯ isn't in C major, so this chord comes from outside the key. It belongs to A harmonic minor: A minor with its seventh note raised from G to G♯. That means C augmented isn't a wrong-sounding accident. Its G♯ sits just one half step below A, the home note of A minor, so the chord leans toward Am and wants to move there. That pull is what it's for.

How the colours show a chord

On this site, every chord's notes are coloured by their job. The root is darkest, then the 3rd, then the 5th, and the 7th (if there is one) is lightest. That's true on the keyboards, the guitar diagrams, the staves and the note lines. It means you can spot the root of any chord at a glance, even one you've never seen. The colour family also changes with the octave, so a high C and a low C look related but not identical.

Part 2: Going deeper

Seventh chords: one more third

If a triad is two thirds stacked, the obvious next step is to stack a third one. Keep skipping letters: after C, E, G comes B. That new top note is seven letters above the root (C up to B), so it's called the seventh, and a four-note chord built this way is a seventh chord.

On the staff, the seventh just adds a fourth rung: line, line, line, line. Which kind of triad you start from, and which size of seventh you add on top, gives five kinds of seventh chord. Here they are, from the softest to the most tense:

  • Major 7th (Cmaj7): major triad + a major 7th on top. Soft and dreamy, the sound of a lot of jazz ballads and bossa nova.
  • Dominant 7th (G7): major triad + a minor 7th on top. Bluesy and restless: it wants to move to another chord.
  • Minor 7th (Am7): minor triad + a minor 7th. Mellow and warm, less sad than a plain minor chord.
  • Half-diminished (Bm7♭5): diminished triad + a minor 7th. Moody, leaning forward.
  • Diminished 7th (G♯°7): three minor thirds stacked. The most tense of all.

Meet the chord: G♯°7

G♯°7 is built entirely from minor thirds: G♯–B–D–F. Its root, G♯, isn't in C major. It comes from A harmonic minor, where the seventh note is raised from G to G♯. That means G♯°7 is built on the note just below A, so it points straight at Am: every one of its notes is only a half step or a whole step away from a note of Am. That's why it sounds so tense, and why it resolves so neatly.

Sixth chords

Instead of a seventh, you can add a sixth: the note one letter lower than the seventh. C6 is C with A added: C–E–G–A. The difference is small on paper but big in sound. A 7th adds pull, a feeling that something should happen next. A 6th adds sweetness, a feeling that everything is fine. That's why sixth chords are the sound of old-time swing, and of a cheerful last chord.

On the staff, the 6th sits in the space just above the fifth, right next to it, rather than on the next line up. That's the one place in these chords where the ladder breaks: the 6th isn't a third above the fifth, it's only a step.

Suspended chords

A suspended chord takes out the third and puts a different note in its place: either the 4th (for Csus4) or the 2nd (for Csus2). Remember, the third is the note that makes a chord major or minor. Take it away and the chord is neither. It sounds open and unfinished, like a question, and it usually moves back to the plain chord, with the 4th falling to the 3rd or the 2nd rising to it.

The dotted lines on the staff follow each note from one chord to the next. Only the middle note moves: F steps down to E, and D steps up to E. The root and fifth stay still.

The augmented 7th

Add a minor 7th to an augmented triad and you get the augmented 7th: Caug7 (C–E–G♯–B♭). Think of it as a dominant 7th chord (C7) with its fifth pushed up a half step, from G to G♯. A dominant 7th already wants to move; raising the fifth gives it one more note that's straining to go somewhere.

Meet the chords: C7 and Caug7

C7 and Caug7 both have B♭, which isn't in C major, and Caug7 also has G♯. They belong to the key of F major, where C7 is the V7 chord. That means both chords point at F: E wants to rise to F, and the raised G♯ in Caug7 wants to rise to A, the third of F. The augmented version just pulls harder.

Arpeggios: a chord one note at a time

So far, every chord has been played with all its notes at once. But you don't have to. Play the same notes one after another, usually from the bottom up, and you get an arpeggio. The word comes from the Italian for "harp", because that's how a harp plays a chord: one string after another.

The important idea is this: an arpeggio is still the chord. It has exactly the same notes, with the same root, third and fifth. The only thing that changes is when you play them. Here's the C chord both ways on a staff. In the first bar, the three notes are stacked straight up and down, so they sound together. In the next bars, the same three notes are spread out from left to right, so they sound one after another.

Compare the colours: the C in the chord and the C in the arpeggio are the same dark root colour, and so on for the third and the fifth. Same notes, same jobs. The staff simply turns "up and down" into "left to right".

Why does this matter? Because once you hear it, you'll notice that most music is full of arpeggios:

  • Melodies often jump between the notes of the chord underneath them. When a tune sounds like it "fits" the harmony, it's usually landing on chord notes.
  • Guitarists pick through a chord shape string by string instead of strumming it. That fingerpicked sound is an arpeggio.
  • Pianists play broken chords in the left hand to keep the music moving without hammering the same block chord over and over.
  • Your ear still hears the chord, because each note keeps ringing in your memory after it's played. By the third note, you've heard the whole chord.

Try it The C arpeggio, up the note line

  • What you'll hear: C, E, G, then the C above, one at a time, the way the rows go.
  • Listen for: the C chord appearing note by note. By the time you reach G, you can hear the whole chord, even though you never played two notes together.
  • That's: an arpeggio: a chord spread out in time. Play it slowly, then faster and faster, and it blurs back into a chord.

Every chord has an arpeggio, and it always follows the chord's own recipe. A minor chord's arpeggio has a minor third at the bottom; a seventh chord's arpeggio has four notes instead of three.

To sum up: a chord tells you which notes to play. Playing them together or one at a time is your choice, and both are the same chord.

Part 3: For the professional

These are the details working musicians run into: different ways of writing the same chord, and chords with unusual shapes.

One chord, many names

Chord symbols aren't fully standard. The same chord can be written several ways, depending on the book or the player:

ChordAlso written
half-diminishedBm7♭5 = Bø7 = Bm7♭5
diminished 7thG♯°7 = G♯dim7
augmentedC+ = Caug = C(♯5)
major 7thCmaj7 = CΔ7 = CM7
minorAm = Amin = A−

This site writes them the way the badges show, so every page matches the app.

Chords that split the octave evenly

Two chords are perfectly symmetrical. The augmented triad stacks major thirds, 4 half steps each, so three of them make exactly one octave. The diminished 7th stacks minor thirds, 3 half steps each, so four make one octave. On the circle of fifths, the augmented triad is an even triangle and the diminished 7th is a perfect square.

Because they're symmetrical, these chords have no single obvious root. C+, E+ and A♭+ are the same three keys on a piano, just with different names. That makes them great "pivot" chords that can lead almost anywhere.

On the staff they look different, because each is spelled from its own root and stacked line, line, line. But play them and they're the same three piano keys (G♯ = A♭, B♯ = C). The spelling tells you which way the chord is about to move; the sound alone doesn't.

A chord's notes, not its name, decide its job

C6 and Am7 contain exactly the same four notes: C → E → G → A. Which one you hear depends on the lowest note and on the key around it. So what a chord does in a song (sound like home, move away, pull back) depends on its notes and their surroundings, not on its symbol. That's the subject of the Chord Functions page.

Using it at the instrument

  • Build every chord from its root. Find the root, count up a third, then another third. On piano, that's "play one, skip one, play one" with the white keys in C major.
  • Switch one note at a time. Play C, then lower the E to E♭ for Cm, then raise it to F for Csus4. You'll feel how one note changes the whole mood.
  • Add the 7th last. Learn a triad first, then add the 7th on top. On guitar, that usually means lifting one finger or adding one.
  • Say the chord as you play it: "root, third, fifth". Naming the notes builds the picture faster than memorising shapes.
  • Play arpeggios slowly, one note per beat. Then play the same notes together as a chord.
  • In Warbling Wren, pick a chord type and tap through all 12 roots: the colours show the same recipe on every root.
