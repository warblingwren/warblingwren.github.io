How Chord Progressions Are Related

Chord progressions aren't a list of separate things to memorise. They're a family. Many famous progressions are the same chords in a different order, the same pattern in a different key, or a familiar progression with one chord swapped. Once you see the connections, learning one progression teaches you several. This page starts from zero and works up to the ideas professionals use. Read as far as you like.

Part 1: The basics

A few words you'll need

  • Progression. A series of chords played in order, usually repeated. I – IV – V means "play the I chord, then the IV chord, then the V chord".
  • Roman numerals. Chords are numbered by the scale note they're built on. In C major: I = C, ii = Dm, iii = Em, IV = F, V = G, vi = Am. Uppercase is a major chord, lowercase is minor. Numerals let one progression work in every key: I – IV – V is C – F – G in C, and A – D – E in A.
  • Home. The chord the music feels settled on, the I chord (or i in a minor key).

Six simple relationships

All the examples below are in C major or A minor, so you can compare the actual chords.

  • Same chords. Two progressions that are exactly the same, just with different names. Country's I–IV–V and rock & roll's three-chord I–IV–V are both C – F – G. Different genres, same chords. What makes them sound different is rhythm, tempo, instruments and style.
  • Rotation. The same chords in the same order, but starting from a different chord. Like a song on a loop that you join halfway through. I–V–vi–IV is C – G – Am – F. Start the same loop on Am and you get vi–IV–I–V: Am – F – C – G. Same four chords, same order, but now the loop begins on a minor chord, so it feels sadder.
  • Variant. The same progression with one small change. The 12-bar blues "quick change" is the regular 12-bar blues with bar 2 changed from I7 to IV7. Everything else is identical.
  • Parallel minor. The same pattern of numerals, moved from a major key to a minor key on the same home note. The 12-bar blues (I7 – IV7 – V7) becomes the minor blues (i7 – iv7 – V7). The shape is the same, but the mood is darker.
  • Extension. A progression with extra chords added in front. ii–V–I is Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7. Put two more chords in front, Em7 and Am7, and you get iii–vi–ii–V. The new chords follow the same pattern as the old ones, so the progression just gets longer.
  • Contains. A progression built from another one. The 12-bar blues uses exactly the three chords of I–IV–V, each turned into a 7th chord (C7, F7, G7) and spread across 12 bars.

Try it on WarblingWren: play I–V–vi–IV, then vi–IV–I–V, and listen for the same four chords feeling brighter or darker depending on where the loop starts.

Part 2: Going deeper

The "four chord" family

Five of the progressions on this site use the same four chords in C: C, G, Am and F. They're the I, V, vi and IV chords: the home chord, the two chords next to it on the circle of fifths, and its relative minor.

Progression In C / A minor How it relates to I–V–vi–IV
I–V–vi–IV (pop "axis")C – G – Am – FThe starting point

I–vi–IV–V ('50s)C – Am – F – GSame four chords, different order

Look at vi–IV–I–V and i–VI–III–VII: in C and A minor they're exactly the same chords in the same order. The only difference is which chord your ear treats as home. A major key and its relative minor share all their notes, so the same four chords can be written either way. In practice, the melody and the chord that lands on the strongest beats decide which reading you hear.

Rotation versus reordering

A rotation keeps the order and moves the starting point. Reordering shuffles the chords.

  • Rotation keeps every chord change the same, so the progression keeps its character, but the ending moves. I–V–vi–IV ends F → C when it loops, a calm "Amen" ending. Its rotation vi–IV–I–V ends G → Am when it loops, a "plot twist" ending, where you expect C and get Am.
  • Reordering changes the chord changes themselves. The '50s progression C – Am – F – G moves gently (C to Am to F share notes), then pushes (F to G) into a strong G → C return. I–V–vi–IV starts with a stronger jump (C to G). Same ingredients, different recipe.

Contains: progressions inside progressions

  • The 12-bar blues contains I–IV–V. Same three roots, made into 7th chords and arranged as 4 bars of I7, 2 of IV7, 2 of I7, then V7 – IV7 – I7 – V7.
  • The Canon contains I–V–vi–IV. Canon: C – G – Am – Em – F. Take out the Em and you have the pop axis, C – G – Am – F.
  • Minor pop contains the minor rock climb. i–VI–VII is Am – F – G. Minor pop, i–VI–III–VII, is Am – F – C – G: the same climb with C added.

Extension: building backwards by fifths

The strongest way to arrive at a chord is from the chord a fifth above it: G7 → C. You can keep adding chords in front, each a fifth above the last, and every step pulls toward the next:

Each new chord is one step further back around the circle of fifths, so you can see the chain on WarblingWren's circle.

Substitution: swapping one chord for a close relative

Some chords can stand in for others because they share two of their three notes. Swap one, and you get a new progression that does the same job with a different colour.

  • iii for I. I–vi–ii–V (Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7) becomes iii–vi–ii–V (Em7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7). Em and C share E and G, so Em7 starts the turnaround on a softer version of home.
  • IV for ii. I–vi–ii–V (C – Am – Dm – G) becomes the '50s progression, I–vi–IV–V (C – Am – F – G). Dm and F share F and A, so both lead into G the same way.
  • II7 for ii. Turn the ii chord (Dm) into a major chord with a 7th (D7), and it stops being an "away" chord and becomes a chord that points hard at G. That's the country progression I–II7–V7 (C – D7 – G7): the I–ii–V idea, sharpened.

Parallel major and minor

Keep the numerals and switch the key from major to minor on the same home note:

Notice that in the minor blues and the minor ii–V–i, the V chord stays major (E7). A major V contains G♯, a half step below the home note A, which gives it a strong pull home. The minor i–iv–v keeps a minor v (Em), so its pull is softer and folkier.

Variants of the same form

  • Quick change. The 12-bar blues with IV7 in bar 2 instead of I7.
  • 8-bar blues. The same three chords as the 12-bar (I7, IV7, V7) in a shorter, eight-bar form: I7 – V7 – IV7 – IV7 – I7 – V7 – I7 – V7.

Same shape, different home

Sometimes the chords of one progression appear inside another, heard from a different home. The end of the 12-bar blues goes G7 – F7 – C7 (V7 – IV7 – I7). The rock progression I–♭VII–IV in C is C – B♭ – F. Measure that from F, and it's the same shape, V – IV – I. The rock progression is the blues turnaround heard as if the V chord were home, which is a big part of why it sounds bluesy and unresolved.

Mirror images

The minor rock climb i–VI–VII (Am – F – G) walks up from F to G and back to Am. The Andalusian cadence i–VII–VI–V (Am – G – F – E) walks down through the same G and F and keeps going to E. Same chords, opposite direction, opposite feeling: one rises with energy, the other sinks with drama.

Part 3: For the professional

Levels of equivalence

"Same progression" can mean several different things. It helps to be precise about which one you mean:

  • Transposition. Roman numerals already factor out key. C – F – G and A – D – E are the same progression, I–IV–V.
  • Cyclic equivalence. For loops, rotations are the same sequence of chord changes with a different downbeat, a "necklace" rather than a string. I–V–vi–IV and vi–IV–I–V are one necklace. The '50s progression and I–IV–vi–V are different necklaces over the same chord set.
  • Set equivalence. Same chords, any order. All five four-chord progressions above belong to one set, {I, IV, V, vi}.
  • Relative re-reading. One pitch-level sequence, two Roman-numeral readings: vi–IV–I–V in C and i–VI–III–VII in A minor. Which reading is right isn't in the chords. It comes from the melody, the bass, metric weight and what the song does at its cadences. Pop loops are often deliberately ambiguous between the two.
  • Functional equivalence. Progressions with the same sequence of tonic, subdominant and dominant functions. I–vi–ii–V, iii–vi–ii–V and I–vi–IV–V all run T – T – S – D (counting vi and iii as tonic stand-ins), so they're interchangeable at the level of phrase structure.

Substitution as transformation

Neo-Riemannian theory names the single-note moves that turn one triad into another while keeping two common tones:

  • R (relative): C ↔ Am. One note moves by a whole step (G ↔ A). That's the I/vi and IV/ii substitution.
  • L (leading-tone exchange): C ↔ Em. One note moves by a half step (C ↔ B). That's the I/iii substitution.
  • P (parallel): C ↔ Cm. The 3rd moves by a half step (E ↔ E♭). That's the parallel-minor relationship, and the source of borrowed chords such as the minor iv.

The site's substitution relationships are single R or L moves on one chord. Parallel-minor pairs are P applied to the tonic (with the rest of the key following). Seen this way, the four-chord family is a small neighbourhood in triad space: C, Am, F and G are linked by R (C ↔ Am), L (F ↔ Am) and fifth relations (F – C – G).

Quality alteration and secondary function

Changing a chord's quality without changing its root can change its function. ii (Dm) is subdominant. II7 (D7) is V7/V, a dominant aimed at G. The roots of I–ii–V and I–II7–V7 are identical, and only the third and seventh change, yet the second progression has two dominants in a row, a "chain of dominants" in miniature. The same logic turns I–vi–ii–V into I–VI7–II7–V7 (C – A7 – D7 – G7), a classic ragtime and swing reharmonisation.

Rotation, root motion and cadence

Rotating a loop never changes its cycle of root motions, only where the cycle starts. I–V–vi–IV moves up a fifth, up a step, down a third, then up a fifth to return. Every rotation has the same four moves. What rotation changes is which move becomes the cadence at the loop point:

  • I–V–vi–IV turns around on IV → I (plagal).
  • vi–IV–I–V turns around on V → vi (deceptive).
  • IV–I–V–vi turns around on vi → IV.
  • V–vi–IV–I turns around on I → V (half).

That's why rotations of one loop can sound like four different songs.

Modal re-centering

I–♭VII–IV and the blues turnaround V–IV–I share interval content, because both are a descending whole step followed by an ascending fifth. In C, I–♭VII–IV is Mixolydian (C D E F G A B♭): the major-key collection centred on its own 5th degree. So the progression is the parent key's V–IV–I with the tonic moved to V. The same re-centering explains the Andalusian cadence's double life: it ends on V in A minor, or on I in E Phrygian.

Practical uses

  • Learning. Learn one member of a family thoroughly, then learn the others as differences. Rotations need no new chords at all.
  • Reharmonising. Swap a chord for its R or L neighbour, alter a chord's quality to make it a secondary dominant, or extend backwards by fifths to approach a target more strongly.
  • Songwriting. Keep the loop and change the rotation to move between verse and chorus moods. Switch to the parallel minor for a bridge. Use a contained progression (I–IV–V inside the 12-bar) to simplify a section.
  • Ear training. When you hear a new progression, ask which relationship ties it to one you know. Usually there is one.
