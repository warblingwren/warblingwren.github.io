How Chord Progressions Are Related

Chord progressions aren't a list of separate things to memorise. They're a family. Many famous progressions are the same chords in a different order, the same pattern in a different key, or a familiar progression with one chord swapped. Once you see the connections, learning one progression teaches you several. This page starts from zero and works up to the ideas professionals use. Read as far as you like.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. Nearly every progression on this page is built from them, which makes it easy to see when two progressions share chords.

How to read the diagrams: on the keyboard, the coloured keys are the chord's notes. On the guitar, each dot is a finger on a fret, an open circle above the nut is an open string, and × means don't play that string. The darkest dot is the chord's root, the note it's named after. Like the knots in wood, these voicings have a character of their own.

Part 1: The basics

A few words you'll need

  • Progression. A series of chords played in order, usually repeated. I – IV – V means "play the I chord, then the IV chord, then the V chord".
  • Roman numerals. Chords are numbered by the scale note they're built on. In C major: I = C, ii = Dm, iii = Em, IV = F, V = G, vi = Am. Uppercase is a major chord, lowercase is minor. Numerals let one progression work in every key: I – IV – V is C – F – G in C, and A – D – E in A.
  • Home. The chord the music feels settled on, the I chord (or i in a minor key).
  • 7th chord. A chord with a fourth note added on top. C7 is C with B♭ added: C–E–G–B♭. The 7th makes a chord richer or more restless.

All the examples below are in C major or A minor, so you can compare the actual chords.

The same chords in the same order, but starting from a different chord. Like a song on a loop that you join halfway through. I–V–vi–IV is C – G – Am – F. 4. Parallel minor

The same pattern of numerals, moved from a major key to a minor key on the same home note. The 12-bar blues (I7 – IV7 – V7) becomes the minor blues (i7 – iv7 – V7). The shape is the same, but the mood is darker.

Meet the chord: E7

E7 (E–G♯–B–D) is the V7 chord of A minor, but it isn't built from the plain A minor scale, because of its G♯. It comes from A harmonic minor: the minor scale with its 7th note raised from G to G♯. That raised note sits a half step below A, so E7 pulls hard toward home.

Look at vi–IV–I–V and i–VI–III–VII: in C and A minor they're exactly the same chords in the same order. The only difference is which chord your ear treats as home. A major key and its relative minor share all their notes, so the same four chords can be written either way. In practice, the melody and the chord that lands on the strongest beats decide which reading you hear.

Rotation versus reordering

A rotation keeps the order and moves the starting point. Reordering shuffles the chords.

  • Rotation keeps every chord change the same, so the progression keeps its character, but the ending moves. I–V–vi–IV ends F → C when it loops, a calm "Amen" ending. Its rotation vi–IV–I–V ends G → Am when it loops, a "plot twist" ending, where you expect C and get Am.
  • Reordering changes the chord changes themselves. The '50s progression C – Am – F – G moves gently (C to Am to F share notes), then pushes (F to G) into a strong G → C return. I–V–vi–IV starts with a stronger jump (C to G). Same ingredients, different recipe.

Contains: progressions inside progressions

  • The 12-bar blues contains I–IV–V. Same three roots, made into 7th chords and arranged as 4 bars of I7, 2 of IV7, 2 of I7, then V7 – IV7 – I7 – V7.
  • The Canon contains I–V–vi–IV. Canon: C – G – Am – Em – F. Take out the Em and you have the pop axis, C – G – Am – F.
  • Minor pop contains the minor rock climb. i–VI–VII is Am – F – G. Minor pop, i–VI–III–VII, is Am – F – C – G: the same climb with C added.

Extension: building backwards by fifths

The strongest way to arrive at a chord is from the chord a fifth above it: G7 → C. You can keep adding chords in front, each a fifth above the last, and every step pulls toward the next:

ProgressionIn CAdded in front
V–IG7 – C–
ii–V–IDm7 – G7 – Cmaj7Dm7, a fifth above G
vi–ii–V(–I)Am7 – Dm7 – G7 – CAm7, a fifth above D
iii–vi–ii–V(–I)Em7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7 – CEm7, a fifth above A

Each new chord is one step further back around the circle of fifths, so you can see the chain on the circle in Warbling Wren.

Substitution: swapping one chord for a close relative

Some chords can stand in for others because they share two of their three notes. Swap one, and you get a new progression that does the same job with a different colour.

  • iii for I. I–vi–ii–V (Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7) becomes iii–vi–ii–V (Em7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7). Em and C share E and G, so Em7 starts the turnaround on a softer version of home.
  • IV for ii. I–vi–ii–V (C – Am – Dm – G) becomes the '50s progression, I–vi–IV–V (C – Am – F – G). Dm and F share F and A, so both lead into G the same way.
  • II7 for ii. Turn the ii chord (Dm) into a major chord with a 7th (D7), and it stops being an "away" chord and becomes a chord that points hard at G. That's the country progression I–II7–V7 (C – D7 – G7): the I–ii–V idea, sharpened.

Same shape, different home

Sometimes the chords of one progression appear inside another, heard from a different home. The end of the 12-bar blues goes G7 – F7 – C7 (V7 – IV7 – I7). The rock progression I–♭VII–IV in C is C – B♭ – F. Measure that from F, and it's the same shape, V – IV – I. The rock progression is the blues turnaround heard as if the V chord were home, which is a big part of why it sounds bluesy and unresolved.

Meet the chord: B♭

B♭ (B♭–D–F) comes from C Mixolydian: the C major scale with its 7th lowered from B to B♭. Measured from F, it's the IV chord, which is why C – B♭ – F sounds like a blues ending.

Mirror images

The minor rock climb i–VI–VII (Am – F – G) walks up from F to G and back to Am. The Andalusian cadence i–VII–VI–V (Am – G – F – E) walks down through the same G and F and keeps going to E. Same chords, opposite direction, opposite feeling: one rises with energy, the other sinks with drama.

Meet the chord: E

E major (E–G♯–B) comes from A harmonic minor, like E7. Without the 7th it's a plain major triad, the sound that ends every Andalusian cadence.

Part 3: For the professional

This part uses the language players, teachers and theorists use when they compare progressions. Every term is explained as it comes up.

Levels of equivalence

"Same progression" can mean several different things. It helps to be precise about which one you mean:

  • Transposition. Moving everything to a new key. Roman numerals already factor out key: C – F – G and A – D – E are the same progression, I–IV–V.
  • Cyclic equivalence. For loops, rotations are the same sequence of chord changes with a different downbeat (the first, strongest beat of the loop): a "necklace" rather than a string. I–V–vi–IV and vi–IV–I–V are one necklace. The '50s progression and I–IV–vi–V are different necklaces over the same chord set.
  • Set equivalence. Same chords, any order. All five four-chord progressions above belong to one set, {I, IV, V, vi}.
  • Relative re-reading. One sequence of actual chords, two Roman-numeral readings: vi–IV–I–V in C and i–VI–III–VII in A minor. Which reading is right isn't in the chords. It comes from the melody, the bass, which chords fall on strong beats (metric weight) and what the song does at its cadences. Pop loops are often deliberately ambiguous between the two.
  • Functional equivalence. Progressions with the same sequence of chord functions: tonic (T, home), subdominant (S, away) and dominant (D, tension). I–vi–ii–V, iii–vi–ii–V and I–vi–IV–V all run T – T – S – D (counting vi and iii as tonic stand-ins), so they're interchangeable at the level of phrase structure.

Substitution as transformation

Neo-Riemannian theory, a branch of modern theory named after the 19th-century theorist Hugo Riemann, names the single-note moves that turn one major or minor chord into another while keeping two notes the same:

  • R (relative): C ↔ Am. One note moves by a whole step (G ↔ A). That's the I/vi and IV/ii substitution.
  • L (leading-tone exchange): C ↔ Em. One note moves by a half step (C ↔ B). That's the I/iii substitution.
  • P (parallel): C ↔ Cm. The 3rd moves by a half step (E ↔ E♭). That's the parallel-minor relationship, and the source of borrowed chords such as the minor iv.

The site's substitution relationships are single R or L moves on one chord. Parallel-minor pairs are P applied to the tonic (with the rest of the key following). Seen this way, the four-chord family is a small neighbourhood in the space of chords: C, Am, F and G are linked by R (C ↔ Am), L (F ↔ Am) and fifth relations (F – C – G).

Meet the chords: Cm and Fm

Both come from C minor (C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭), the parallel minor of C major. Cm is C with its 3rd lowered from E to E♭: a P move. Fm is the iv chord of C minor, borrowed into C major all the time.

Quality alteration and secondary function

Changing a chord's quality (major, minor, 7th) without changing its root can change its function. ii (Dm) is subdominant. II7 (D7) is V7/V, a dominant aimed at G. The roots of I–ii–V and I–II7–V7 are identical, and only the third and seventh change, yet the second progression has two dominants in a row, a "chain of dominants" in miniature. The same logic turns I–vi–ii–V into I–VI7–II7–V7 (C – A7 – D7 – G7), a classic ragtime and swing reharmonisation.

Meet the chord: A7

A7 (A–C♯–E–G) is Am with its 3rd raised from C to C♯ and a 7th added. C♯ comes from D minor's harmonic scale, so A7 becomes the V7 chord of Dm: a secondary dominant pointing at D7.

Rotation, root motion and cadence

Rotating a loop never changes its cycle of root motions, only where the cycle starts. I–V–vi–IV moves up a fifth, up a step, down a third, then up a fifth to return. Every rotation has the same four moves. What rotation changes is which move becomes the cadence (the chord change that closes a phrase) at the loop point:

  • I–V–vi–IV turns around on IV → I (plagal).
  • vi–IV–I–V turns around on V → vi (deceptive).
  • IV–I–V–vi turns around on vi → IV.
  • V–vi–IV–I turns around on I → V (half).

Modal re-centering

I–♭VII–IV and the blues turnaround V–IV–I share the same intervals, because both are a descending whole step followed by an ascending fifth. In C, I–♭VII–IV uses the C Mixolydian scale (C D E F G A B♭): the F major collection centred on its own 5th note. So the progression is the parent key's V–IV–I with home moved to V. The same re-centering explains the Andalusian cadence's double life: it ends on V in A minor, or on I in E Phrygian (the scale of the white keys from E to E).

Practical uses

  • Learning. Learn one member of a family thoroughly, then learn the others as differences. Rotations need no new chords at all.
  • Reharmonising. Choosing new chords for an existing melody. Swap a chord for its R or L neighbour, alter a chord's quality to make it a secondary dominant, or extend backwards by fifths to approach a target more strongly.
  • Songwriting. Keep the loop and change the rotation to move between verse and chorus moods. Switch to the parallel minor for a bridge. Use a contained progression (I–IV–V inside the 12-bar) to simplify a section.
  • Ear training. When you hear a new progression, ask which relationship ties it to one you know. Usually there is one.

Using it at the instrument

  • Learn the four chords C, G, Am, F until you can switch between them without looking. That one set unlocks five progressions on this site.
  • Loop I–V–vi–IV and, without stopping, start counting the loop from Am instead. You've just played a rotation, and you'll hear the mood change.
  • Play I–IV–V and then I–IV–V with the same strumming pattern. Then change only the strumming. Hear how much of a genre is rhythm, not chords.
  • On piano, practise the R and L moves: from C, move one finger up a whole step to get Am, or move your thumb down a half step to get Em.
  • On guitar, add the 7th to each blues chord one at a time (C to C7, F to F7, G to G7) and hear I–IV–V turn into the 12-bar blues.
  • Take any song in a major key and play its chords in the parallel minor (same numerals, minor home). Keep the V chord major for a stronger pull.
  • Before any G chord in a song, try D7 first. Then try turning Am into A7. Hear the "chain of dominants" grow.
  • Play the minor rock climb and the Andalusian cadence back to back (Am – F – G, then Am – G – F – E) to hear the mirror image.
  • In Warbling Wren, every progression can be played from any root and in its relative key. Compare relatives back to back to hear these connections for yourself.
