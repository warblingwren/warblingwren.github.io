Reading Music

Music has been written down in many different ways, and each way was invented to answer a different question. Staff notation says exactly which notes to play and for how long. Guitar tab says where to put your fingers. A chord symbol says which chord to strum and leaves the rest to you. This page walks through all of them, from the simplest to the most complete, and then shows how Warbling Wren draws music and why it does it differently. Once you know what each system is for, any page of music you come across will tell you something useful.

Part 1: The basics

Written music at a glance

Before learning any one system, it helps to see them side by side. Each one shows some things and leaves others out, so the right one depends on what you play and what you need to know.

The first few are the quickest to learn, so this part starts with them. The staff comes next, because almost every other system borrows something from it.

Chord symbols

A chord symbol is a chord's name written above the words of a song or above the music. The letter is the root, the note the chord is built on, and anything after the letter is the chord's type. If there's nothing after the letter, the chord is major. If you know a handful of these, you can play along with most songbooks.

Chord diagrams

A chord diagram is a picture of one chord shape on the guitar neck, standing upright so the strings run from top to bottom. Dots show where your fingers go, a small circle above a string means play it open, and an × means leave it out. Songbooks print a row of these above the music, one for each chord the song uses, so you can learn the shapes before you start. Every chord on this site comes with one, and Reading the Fretboard explains every mark.

Guitar tab

Tablature, or tab, is the most popular way to write guitar music, because it skips note names altogether and tells you exactly where to put your fingers. To read it, picture the guitar lying flat on your lap with its strings facing up. Each of the six lines is a string, with the thinnest string (the high E) at the top and the thickest (the low E) at the bottom. A number on a line is the fret to press on that string, and 0 means play the string open. You read from left to right, one number at a time.

When numbers are stacked on top of each other, you play them together as a chord. If you compare the stacks below with the chord diagrams above, you'll find the same shapes, just turned on their side.

Tab has one big gap: it usually doesn't show rhythm, so it can't tell you how long to hold each note. That's why tab books often print the staff above the tab, so you get the rhythm from the staff and the finger positions from the tab. If you're learning a song from tab alone, listen to a recording to hear the timing.

The staff: five lines for pitch

Standard notation is written on a staff (also called a stave): five lines with four spaces between them. Each line and each space is one note name, and the higher a note sits on the staff, the higher it sounds. Moving from a line to the next space up is one step up the letters: C to D, D to E, and so on. That means a run of notes climbing up the staff is a run of notes climbing up the keyboard.

Clefs: what the lines mean

A staff on its own doesn't say which note each line is. The clef at the start of the staff does. The two you'll meet most often are the treble clef, for higher notes (the right hand on piano, melody instruments, most singers), and the bass clef, for lower notes (the left hand on piano, bass guitar, cello). Each clef marks one line with a note, and every other line and space follows from it.

ClefNameHow it marks its note
Treble clef, or G clefits curl wraps around the second line from the bottom, which makes that line G
Bass clef, or F clefits two dots sit either side of the second line from the top, which makes that line F

Musicians remember the lines and spaces with short phrases. On the treble staff, the lines from the bottom up are E G B D F (Every Good Boy Does Fine), and the spaces spell F A C E, which reads as the word FACE.

On the bass staff, the lines from the bottom up are G B D F A (Good Boys Do Fine Always), and the spaces are A C E G (All Cows Eat Grass).

Piano music uses both at once. The treble staff sits above the bass staff, joined by a line at the left, and together they're called the grand staff. Middle C (C4) sits exactly between them on its own short line, so the right hand reads upward from it and the left hand reads downward. Reading the Keyboard shows each of these notes under your fingers.

Ledger lines: notes above and below

When a note is too high or too low for the five lines, it gets short extra lines of its own, called ledger lines. If you count them the same way as the staff lines, line, space, line, space, you can name any note, however far off the staff it goes. Middle C on the treble staff is the most common example: one ledger line below the staff.

Sharps, flats and naturals

The lines and spaces only name the white keys. To reach a black key, a sign called an accidental is written just in front of the note. Each one moves the note by a half step, the smallest step in Western music: one key on the piano, one fret on the guitar. The sign applies to that note for the rest of the bar, so you don't have to read it again every time the note repeats.

SignNameIt means

double sharptwo half steps higher (rare)
double flattwo half steps lower (rare)

Part 2: Going deeper

How long each note lasts

A note's position on the staff gives its pitch, and its shape gives its length. Lengths are counted in beats, the steady pulse you tap your foot to. Each shape lasts half as long as the one above it, and every note has a matching rest: a sign for silence that lasts just as long.

NoteRestNameLasts
whole note4 beats
half note2 beats
quarter note1 beat
eighth note½ beat
sixteenth note¼ beat

If you look at the shapes from top to bottom, you'll find each step adds one thing: the whole note is an open oval, the half note adds a stem, the quarter note fills the oval in, and each flag after that halves the length again. In British English these are called the semibreve, minim, crotchet, quaver and semiquaver.

Eighth and sixteenth notes are usually joined in groups by a thick line called a beam instead of each having its own flag. A beam means exactly the same as the flags; it just groups the notes into beats so they're easier to read.

Counting a rhythm

The drawings below show rhythm on its own, on a single line, with no pitch. Each note's distance from the next shows how long it lasts, and the row underneath is how musicians count it out loud. Say the numbers in time with a steady beat: a bold count is where a note starts, a count in brackets is a note still sounding, and a grey count is a rest. Eighth notes are counted with "and" between the beats, written &.

Try it Clap a rhythm

  • Listen for: the silence on beat 2 of the second bar, where the rest is, and the two quick claps on "3 &".
  • That's: reading rhythm: the shapes tell you when to play and when to wait.

Time signatures

Music is divided into bars (also called measures) by vertical bar lines, and every bar holds the same number of beats. The time signature at the start says how many. It's two numbers stacked up: the top number is how many beats are in each bar, and the bottom number is which note gets one beat, where 4 means a quarter note and 8 means an eighth note.

TimeSayIt feels like
"four four"four beats to a bar; most pop, rock and blues
"common time"another way to write 4/4
"three four"three beats to a bar; a waltz: ONE two three
"six eight"six eighth notes in two groups of three: ONE two three FOUR five six
"cut time"2/2: two half-note beats to a bar; marches and fast songs

Dots and ties

Two signs make a note longer. A dot after a note adds half its length again, so a dotted half note lasts 2 + 1 = 3 beats, and a dotted quarter lasts 1 + ½ = 1½ beats. A tie is a curved line joining two notes of the same pitch: you play the first and hold it through the second without playing again. Ties are how a note keeps sounding across a bar line.

A curved line between two different notes looks like a tie but isn't one. It's a slur, and it means play the notes smoothly, joined together, with no gap between them.

Key signatures

Instead of writing the same sharp or flat in front of every note, music in most keys puts them once, at the start of every staff, in a key signature. A sharp or flat in the key signature applies to that note in every octave, all the way through the music, unless an accidental changes it. That means the key signature also tells you the key: one sharp (F♯) is usually G major or E minor, and one flat (B♭) is usually F major or D minor.

Each key has its own key signature, and the Circle of Fifths page shows how they follow one another: each step around the circle adds one sharp or one flat.

Tempo: how fast

The speed of the beat is the tempo, measured in beats per minute (BPM). A metronome mark above the first bar gives it exactly: a quarter note = 120 means 120 quarter-note beats a minute, two every second. Older music uses Italian words instead, which give a speed and a mood together.

How loud to play is written with letters from Italian: p for piano (soft) and f for forte (loud), with m for mezzo (medium) in between. Doubling a letter makes it more extreme. Long wedge shapes called hairpins show the music getting gradually louder or softer.

Articulation: how each note is played

Small marks above or below a note head change how that one note is played. They don't change its pitch or its written length, only its attack and how it ends, and that's often the difference between a part sounding mechanical and sounding alive.

Lead sheets and chord charts

A lead sheet puts the melody on a single treble staff and the chord symbols above it. It's the standard format for jazz and for songwriters, because it shows the tune and the harmony on one page and leaves the arrangement to whoever plays it. A chord chart goes one step further and drops the melody: just the bars, with the chords in each. Band musicians read chord charts to learn a song's shape quickly.

The chart below is how a chord chart lays out a song, four bars to a line. Each box is one bar, and the number in its corner is the bar number. This one is the 12-bar blues in A, three lines of four bars that repeat for the whole song.

Part 3: For the professional

These systems write music as relationships instead of fixed notes, so the same page works in any key. They're how musicians talk about harmony, call out changes on a gig and train their ears.

Roman numerals

Roman numerals name each chord by its position in the key instead of by its letter. The chord built on the first note of the scale is I, the chord on the fourth note is IV, and so on. Upper case means major, lower case means minor, and a small circle means diminished. Because the numbers describe distances, the same numerals describe the same progression in every key.

NumeralsIn CIn G
I–V–vi–IVC → G → Am → FG → D → Em → C
ii–V–IDm → G → CAm → D → G
I–IV–VC → F → GG → C → D

Every theory page on this site writes chords this way, and Chord Functions explains what job each numeral does in a key.

The Nashville number system

Session musicians in Nashville took the same idea and made it quicker to write: plain numbers instead of numerals, with a minus sign (or a small m) for minor. So I–V–vi–IV becomes 1 5 6− 4. A Nashville chart lists the numbers bar by bar, and if the singer wants the song in a different key, nobody rewrites anything; the band simply plays the same numbers from a new 1.

Solfège

Solfège gives each note of the scale a syllable you can sing: do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti, then do again. There are two ways to use it. In movable do, used in most English-speaking countries, do is always the first note of whatever key you're in, so the syllables work like numbers you can sing. In fixed do, used across much of Europe and Latin America, do always means C, and the syllables are simply the note names.

Syllabledoremifasollati

Singers use movable do to hear how each note relates to the key: mi always sounds like the bright third of a major key, and ti always sounds like it's pulling up to do. That means if you can sing the syllables, you can work out a melody in any key.

C clefs: alto and tenor

A few instruments read a third kind of clef, the C clef. Its two curves point at middle C, and it can sit on different lines, so whichever line it points to becomes middle C. The viola reads the alto clef nearly all the time, and the cello, bassoon and trombone switch to the tenor clef for their higher notes, which keeps those notes on the staff instead of on a ladder of ledger lines.

The piano roll

Recording software and music apps show notes on a piano roll, named after the punched paper rolls that played old self-playing pianos. To read one, picture a keyboard standing on its end down the left side: each row is one key, so higher rows are higher notes. Time runs from left to right, and each note is a bar that starts when the note starts and is as long as the note lasts. The numbers along the top count the bars and the beats.

A piano roll shows rhythm and pitch as plainly as a drawing can, which is why software uses it, but it can't show which key you're in or which way a note is spelled (C♯ and D♭ share a row). That makes it good for editing a recording and poor for learning theory.

Using it at the instrument

  • Learn the treble lines and spaces first (E G B D F and F A C E), then the bass (G B D F A and A C E G). Name the notes of a page out loud before you play it.
  • Clap the rhythm of a new piece before you play the notes, counting out loud.
  • With tab, find a recording and listen while you read, because tab rarely shows the rhythm.
  • Write a song you know as chord symbols, then as Roman numerals, then play it in a new key from the numerals alone.
  • Sing a major scale in solfège, then sing a simple tune you know in the syllables.

How Warbling Wren writes music

Almost every system above was designed for black ink on paper, long before screens, and for performers who already know the theory behind the notes. Warbling Wren is built for a screen and for people learning how harmony works, so it draws music to answer a different question: not just which notes to play, but why they sound the way they do. It still uses the staff, the keyboard and the guitar neck, but it adds a few things that printed music can't.

Every name is a badge

On this site, every key, note, chord and numeral is written as a coloured badge, and each kind has its own shape, so you can tell at a glance whether a letter means a single note, a whole chord or a key. Each of the twelve notes has its own colour, and it keeps that colour everywhere: C is the same colour as a note, as a chord and as a key.

Colour tells you a note's octave and its job

Inside drawings, colour carries two more pieces of information. Each octave has its own colour family, so a low note and a high note with the same name look related but different. Inside a chord, the shade shows each note's job: the root is the darkest, then the 3rd, the 5th and the 7th, each a step lighter. That means you can see the shape of a chord, which note it's built on and how it's spread across octaves, before reading a single name.

The names are written inside the notes

On a printed staff, you have to work out each note from its line or space. On this site's staves, every note head carries its name, so the staff becomes something you can learn from instead of something you have to decode first. And because the note heads use the same colours as the keys and frets drawn underneath them, the staff, the keyboard and the guitar neck read as one picture.

Distance drawn as distance

The staff hides how far apart two notes really are: E to F and F to G look like the same one-step move, even though one is a half step and the other a whole step. This site's note line fixes that. It's the keyboard straightened out, with every key, white or black, the same width, so the distance between two dots is exactly the distance between the notes. That makes intervals, chord shapes and scale patterns something you can see and count.

Enhanced lead sheets and chord charts

A printed chord chart only names each chord, which is fine if you already know every shape, and a wall if you don't. Wherever a lead sheet or chord chart appears on Warbling Wren, it uses the same grid of bars as the plain chart in Part 2, but each bar holds the full chord card you've seen throughout this site, in the same layout every time: the chord badge at the top, the notes in the chord, the piano keyboard, then the guitar fretboard. That means the chart becomes a complete lesson: the song's shape, and how to play every chord in it, on one page.

You read it exactly like the plain chart: left to right, four bars to a line, with the bar number in each corner. Because every card uses the same colours, a chord that comes back looks identical each time, and when two neighbouring chords share a note, you can see the same coloured key or dot in both cards, which is the note your hand can keep still. Why it's different

Standard notation is a performer's language: precise, compact and complete, but it assumes you already know the theory behind it. Tab and chord symbols are shortcuts that get you playing quickly, but they leave the theory out entirely. Warbling Wren sits between them. Colour shows each note's job, the note line shows real distances, and every drawing is paired with an instrument, so what you read connects straight to what your hands do and what your ears hear. It isn't a replacement for standard notation. It's a bridge to it: once colour and distance have shown you why the notes work, the black dots on a printed page start to make sense on their own.
