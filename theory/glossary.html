Music Theory Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the music theory terms used across WarblingWren, from the absolute basics to the ideas professionals use. Examples are in C major or A minor unless stated otherwise. Jump to a letter, or follow the links to the full explanations.

A

Accidental
A sharp (♯), flat (♭) or natural (♮) sign written in front of a note to raise, lower or restore it for that bar, beyond what the key signature says.
Aeolian mode
Another name for the natural minor scale. On A it is all white keys: A B C D E F G. On C it is C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Altered chord
A chord with one or more notes changed from what the key normally gives it. Turning ii (Dm) into II7 (D7) is an alteration: F becomes F♯ and a 7th is added. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Amen cadence
Another name for the plagal cadence, IV → I (F → C in C), from the two chords sung on "A–men" at the end of many hymns. Learn more: Cadences
Andalusian cadence
The four-chord descent i – VII – VI – V in a minor key: Am – G – F – E in A minor. The sound of flamenco and Spanish folk music. It ends on the V chord, with the bass sinking a half step from F to E. Learn more: Cadences · How Chord Progressions Are Related
Antecedent and consequent
The two halves of a musical question and answer. The antecedent phrase ends on a weaker cadence (often a half cadence), and the consequent answers it with a stronger one (usually an authentic cadence). Together they form a period. Learn more: Cadences
Arpeggio
The notes of a chord played one after another instead of all at once, as in C – E – G – C. From the Italian for "like a harp".
Augmented 7th chord
An augmented chord with a minor 7th added: C – E – G♯ – B♭. Written Caug7 or C7♯5. A tense dominant-type chord that pulls strongly to the next chord.
Augmented chord
A three-note chord with a major 3rd and a raised (augmented) 5th: C – E – G♯. It sounds unsettled and dreamlike, and it splits the octave into three equal parts. Written C+ or Caug.
Authentic cadence
The ending V → I (G → C in C): the strongest, most final cadence. The leading tone (B) rises to the home note, and the bass falls a fifth. Called a perfect cadence in British usage. Learn more: Cadences · What Each Chord Does
Axis progression
The four-chord loop I – V – vi – IV (C – G – Am – F in C), behind many pop songs. Its rotations, such as vi – IV – I – V, use the same four chords starting from a different point. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related

B

Backdoor cadence
A jazz ending that reaches I from the "wrong" side: ♭VII7 → I, often as iv7 → ♭VII7 → I (Fm7 → B♭7 → C in C). A softer alternative to ii – V – I. Learn more: Cadences
Bar
One unit of musical time, holding a set number of beats (four, in most popular music). Also called a measure. The 12-bar blues is twelve of these.
Bass note
The lowest note sounding in a chord. Usually it is the root, but not always: C/E is a C chord with E in the bass. Learn more: Root Motion
Beat
The steady pulse you tap your foot to. Beats are grouped into bars.
Blues
An African-American musical form and style from the early 1900s, built on dominant 7th chords, the 12-bar form and bent "blue" notes. The root of rock & roll, R&B and much of jazz. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Borrowed chord
A chord taken from a closely related scale rather than the song's own key, usually from the parallel major or minor. ♭VII (B♭ in C) and minor iv (Fm in C) are common borrowed chords. Also called modal mixture. Learn more: What Each Chord Does

C

Cadence
The last two chords of a phrase, where the music pauses or stops. Different cadences feel like a full stop, a comma, a question or a surprise. Learn more: Cadences
Cadential six-four
A I chord with the 5th in the bass (C/G in C), placed just before V in a cadence. It works as a decoration of V: the upper notes then fall by step into the V chord. Learn more: Cadences
Chord
Three or more notes sounded together. The basic chord, a triad, stacks every other note of a scale: C – E – G. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Chord function
The job a chord does in a key: home (tonic), away (subdominant) or tension (dominant). Function depends on a chord's place in the key, not just its type. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Chord progression
A series of chords played in order, usually repeated, such as I – IV – V. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Chord quality
The type of chord, set by the distances between its notes: major, minor, diminished, augmented, dominant 7th, and so on.
Chord tone
Any note that belongs to the current chord. For C (C – E – G), the chord tones are C, E and G. Notes outside the chord are non-chord tones.
Chromatic
Using notes outside the key, or moving by half steps. A chromatic scale plays all 12 notes in order. The opposite of diatonic. Learn more: Root Motion
Chromatic mediant
A chord whose root is a third away from the current chord and has the same quality, but which belongs to a different key: C → E, or C → A♭. They share one note, and the change sounds colourful and cinematic. Learn more: Root Motion · How Chord Progressions Are Related
Circle of fifths
A circular map of the 12 keys, each a fifth from its neighbours. It shows key signatures, relative minors, the chords of each key and how closely keys are related. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Circle progression
A progression whose roots move down by fifths, one step at a time around the circle of fifths: iii – vi – ii – V – I. Learn more: Root Motion · The Circle of Fifths
Closely related keys
The keys nearest a given key on the circle of fifths: its relative, the keys one step either side, and their relatives. For C major: A minor, G major, E minor, F major and D minor. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Common tone
A note shared by two chords. Common tones can be held still while the other notes move, which makes a chord change sound smooth. Learn more: Root Motion
Consonance
A combination of notes that sounds stable and at rest, such as a major or minor chord. The opposite of dissonance.
Contrary motion
Two musical lines moving in opposite directions, one up and one down. Common at cadences and an important part of good voice leading. Learn more: Root Motion

D

Deceptive cadence
The ending V → vi (G → Am in C). The music sets up home, then lands somewhere else. Called an interrupted cadence in British usage. Learn more: Cadences
Degree
A note's position in a scale, numbered from 1 (the home note). In C major, E is the 3rd degree and G is the 5th. Roman numerals name the chords built on each degree. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Diatonic
Using only the notes of the key. The diatonic chords of C major are C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am and B°. The opposite of chromatic. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Diminished 7th chord
A diminished chord with another minor 3rd on top: B – D – F – A♭. Every note is three half steps from the next, so it splits the octave into four equal parts. Written B°7 or Bdim7.
Diminished chord
A three-note chord of two minor 3rds: B – D – F. Tense and unstable, it is the vii° chord of a major key. Written B° or Bdim. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Dissonance
A combination of notes that sounds tense and wants to move on, such as the tritone in a dominant 7th chord. Dissonance resolving to consonance is what drives harmony forward. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Dominant
The 5th degree of a scale, and the chord built on it: V (G in C). The tension chord, which pulls strongly back to the tonic. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Dominant 7th chord
A major chord with a minor 7th added: G – B – D – F. Written G7. Its 3rd and 7th form a tritone that wants to resolve, which makes it the strongest pull home in music. In the blues, every chord is a dominant 7th. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Dorian mode
A minor scale with a raised 6th: on D, the white keys D E F G A B C. On C it is C D E♭ F G A B♭. Brighter than natural minor; common in jazz, funk and folk. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Double sharp and double flat
Signs that raise (𝄪) or lower (𝄫) a note by two half steps. They appear when a note has to keep its letter name, as in the 7th of a diminished 7th chord.
Drop D tuning
Guitar tuning with the lowest string lowered from E to D (D A D G B E). It gives a deeper bass and lets you play power chords on the bottom three strings with one finger.

E

Enharmonic
Two names for the same pitch, such as F♯ and G♭, or C♯ and D♭. Which name is used depends on the key. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Equal temperament
The tuning system used by pianos and fretted instruments, which divides the octave into 12 exactly equal half steps. It makes every key equally in tune, at the cost of fifths slightly narrower than pure. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Evaded cadence
A cadence that is set up but sidestepped at the last moment, for example V → I6 (G → C/E) instead of a root-position V → I. Learn more: Cadences
Extended chord
A chord with notes stacked beyond the 7th: 9ths, 11ths and 13ths. C9 is C – E – G – B♭ – D. Common in jazz, funk and R&B.

F

Fifth
The distance from one note to the note five letter names above it, counting the first: C up to G. Seven half steps. The most stable interval after the octave, and the basis of the circle of fifths and of power chords. Learn more: Root Motion · The Circle of Fifths
Flat
The sign ♭, which lowers a note by a half step. B♭ is a half step below B.
Fourth
The distance from one note to the note four letter names above it: C up to F. Five half steps. Going up a fourth lands on the same note name as going down a fifth. Learn more: Root Motion
Fret
A metal strip across a guitar's neck. Pressing a string just behind a fret shortens it and raises its pitch. Each fret is one half step.
Fretboard
The front of a guitar's neck, where you press the strings against the frets. Also called the fingerboard.
Fundamental bass
The line formed by the roots of a progression's chords, whether or not they are actually played in the bass. An idea from Jean-Philippe Rameau (1722), and the basis of Roman-numeral analysis. Learn more: Root Motion

G

Guide tones
The 3rd and 7th of a chord: the two notes that define its quality. In G7 they are B and F. Jazz players outline changes by moving guide tones smoothly from chord to chord. Learn more: Root Motion

H

Half cadence
A phrase ending on the V chord (C → G, or F → G in C). It sounds open and unfinished, like a comma or a question. Called an imperfect cadence in British usage. Learn more: Cadences
Half step
The smallest distance in Western music: from one piano key to the very next one, black or white, or one fret on a guitar. Also called a semitone. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Half-diminished chord
A diminished chord with a minor 7th: B – D – F – A. Written Bø7 or Bm7♭5. The vii chord of a major key, and the ii chord of a minor key (as in the minor ii – V – i). Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Harmonic minor
The natural minor scale with its 7th degree raised a half step. In A: A B C D E F G♯ A. The raised note makes the V chord major (E instead of Em), giving minor keys a strong pull home. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Harmonic rhythm
How often the chords change. A song can have a fast melody over slow harmonic rhythm, or the reverse. Harmonic rhythm often speeds up approaching a cadence. Learn more: Cadences
Harmony
Notes sounding together, and the way chords follow one another. Melody is the tune; harmony is what supports it.

I

Imperfect authentic cadence
A V → I ending that is less final than a perfect authentic cadence, because a chord is inverted, the melody ends on the 3rd or 5th, or vii° replaces V. Learn more: Cadences
Interval
The distance between two notes, named by counting letter names (a third, a fifth) and measured exactly in half steps. Learn more: Root Motion
Inversion
A chord with a note other than the root in the bass. C/E (E in the bass) is first inversion; C/G (G in the bass) is second inversion. Learn more: Root Motion
Ionian mode
Another name for the major scale. On C: C D E F G A B. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths

K

Key
The home base of a piece of music: its home note and the scale built on it. A song in C major keeps returning to C and uses mainly the notes of the C major scale. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Key signature
The sharps or flats written at the start of each line of music, showing which notes are raised or lowered throughout. G major has one sharp (F♯); F major has one flat (B♭). Learn more: The Circle of Fifths

L

Leading tone
The 7th degree of a major (or harmonic minor) scale, a half step below the home note: B in C major, G♯ in A minor. It pulls strongly up to the tonic, and gives the V chord its drive. Learn more: What Each Chord Does · Cadences
Locrian mode
The darkest mode, with a lowered 2nd and 5th: on B, the white keys B C D E F G A. Rare as a home key, because its tonic chord is diminished. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Lydian mode
The brightest mode, a major scale with a raised 4th: on F, the white keys F G A B C D E. On C it is C D E F♯ G A B. Dreamy and floating; common in film music. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths

M

Major 6th chord
A major chord with a major 6th added: C – E – G – A. Written C6. Sweet and settled; common in swing and country endings.
Major 7th chord
A major chord with a major 7th added: C – E – G – B. Written Cmaj7. Smooth and lush; the home chord of the jazz ii – V – I. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Major chord
A three-note chord with a major 3rd and a perfect 5th: C – E – G. Bright and stable. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Major scale
The seven-note scale of whole and half steps in the pattern W W H W W W H: C D E F G A B C. The basis of major keys. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Measure
Another name for a bar.
Mediant
The 3rd degree of a scale, and the chord built on it: iii (Em in C). Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Melodic minor
A minor scale with raised 6th and 7th degrees going up (A B C D E F♯ G♯ A), traditionally returning to natural minor coming down. Jazz uses the ascending form in both directions.
Melody
A sequence of single notes heard as a tune. The part you sing.
Minor 6th chord
A minor chord with a major 6th added: A – C – E – F♯. Written Am6. Moody and sophisticated; common in jazz and gypsy jazz.
Minor 7th chord
A minor chord with a minor 7th added: D – F – A – C. Written Dm7. Mellow and open; the ii chord of the jazz ii – V – I. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Minor chord
A three-note chord with a minor 3rd and a perfect 5th: A – C – E. Darker than a major chord. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Minor plagal cadence
The ending iv → I in a major key (Fm → C in C), using the minor iv chord borrowed from the parallel minor. Its A♭ sinks a half step to G, giving a bittersweet close. Learn more: Cadences
Minor scale
Usually the natural minor scale: whole and half steps in the pattern W H W W H W W. A B C D E F G A, or C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C. See also harmonic minor and melodic minor. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Mixolydian mode
A major scale with a lowered 7th: on G, the white keys G A B C D E F. On C it is C D E F G A B♭. The source of the ♭VII chord heard all over classic rock. Learn more: What Each Chord Does · The Circle of Fifths
Modal mixture
Another name for using borrowed chords: mixing in chords from the parallel major or minor. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Mode
One of seven scales made by starting the major scale's pattern on a different degree: Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian and Locrian. Each has its own mood, from bright to dark. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Modulation
Changing key within a piece. Moving to a neighbouring key on the circle of fifths sounds smooth; moving far away sounds dramatic. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths

N

Natural
The sign ♮, which cancels a sharp or flat and restores the plain note.
Neo-Riemannian theory
A way of describing chord changes as small moves between triads that keep two notes in common. The three basic moves are P (parallel, C ↔ Cm), R (relative, C ↔ Am) and L (leading-tone exchange, C ↔ Em). Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Non-chord tone
A note in the melody that does not belong to the current chord, such as a passing note or a suspension. Non-chord tones add movement and colour.
Note
A single musical sound with a pitch and a length. Also the written symbol for it.

O

Octave
The distance from one note to the next note with the same name, higher or lower: C to the next C. Twelve half steps. The higher note vibrates exactly twice as fast.
Open string
A guitar string played without pressing any fret. In standard tuning the open strings are E A D G B E.
Open tuning
A guitar tuning in which the open strings sound a chord, such as Open G (D G D G B D). Popular for slide guitar and folk.

P

Parallel keys
A major and minor key with the same home note, such as C major and C minor. They have different key signatures (C minor has three flats). Learn more: The Circle of Fifths · How Chord Progressions Are Related
Parallel motion
Two lines moving in the same direction by the same interval. Parallel fifths and octaves are avoided in traditional voice leading because they make the parts lose their independence. Learn more: Root Motion
Passing tone
A non-chord tone that connects two chord tones by step, such as D between C and E.
Pedal point
A note, usually in the bass, held or repeated while the chords above it change. Learn more: Root Motion
Pentatonic scale
A five-note scale. The major pentatonic leaves out the 4th and 7th of the major scale (C D E G A); the minor pentatonic is the same notes from the relative minor (A C D E G). The basis of countless blues, rock and folk melodies. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Perfect authentic cadence
The most final ending: V → I with both chords in root position and the home note in the melody on the last chord. Learn more: Cadences
Period
A pair of phrases that sound like a question and its answer: an antecedent ending on a weaker cadence, then a consequent ending on a strong one. Learn more: Cadences
Phrase
A musical sentence: a short passage that ends with a cadence. Phrases are often four or eight bars long. Learn more: Cadences
Phrygian cadence
A minor-key half cadence, iv6 → V (Dm/F → E in A minor), in which the bass sinks a half step onto the V chord. Solemn and ancient-sounding. Learn more: Cadences
Phrygian mode
A minor scale with a lowered 2nd: on E, the white keys E F G A B C D. On C it is C D♭ E♭ F G A♭ B♭. Dark and Spanish-sounding. In flamenco, the Andalusian cadence is often heard as ending on the home chord of this mode. Learn more: Cadences · The Circle of Fifths
Picardy third
Ending a minor-key piece on a major tonic chord: A major instead of A minor. A moment of light at the end. Learn more: Cadences
Pitch
How high or low a note sounds, set by how fast it vibrates.
Pitch class
All notes with the same name, in any octave: every C, from the lowest to the highest, belongs to pitch class C. Theorists often number them C = 0 up to B = 11. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Plagal cadence
The ending IV → I (F → C in C). Warm and calm, with no leading tone. Also called the Amen cadence. Learn more: Cadences
Power chord
A root and its fifth, often with the root doubled an octave up (C – G – C). Written C5. With no 3rd, it is neither major nor minor; the backbone of rock and metal guitar.
Predominant
Another name for the subdominant function: chords such as IV and ii that lead into the dominant. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Pythagorean comma
The small gap, about a quarter of a semitone, between twelve pure fifths and seven octaves. Equal temperament spreads it across all twelve fifths so the circle of fifths closes. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths

Q

Quick change
A 12-bar blues variant that moves to IV7 in bar 2 before returning to I7. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related

R

Relative key
A major and a minor key that share the same notes and key signature, such as C major and A minor. The relative minor's home note is the 6th degree of the major scale. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths · How Chord Progressions Are Related
Resolution
The move from a tense chord or note to a stable one, such as G7 to C, or B rising to C. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Rhythm changes
The chord progression of George Gershwin's "I Got Rhythm", one of the most-used frameworks in jazz. Its A section is built on the I – vi – ii – V turnaround. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Roman numerals
Numbers I to VII naming chords by the scale degree of their root. Uppercase is major (I, IV, V), lowercase is minor (ii, iii, vi), and ° marks diminished. They let one progression be described in every key. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Root
The note a chord is built on and named after: C is the root of C – E – G. Learn more: Root Motion
Root motion
How far the root moves from one chord to the next: by a fifth (strong), a third (gentle) or a step (bold). Learn more: Root Motion
Root position
A chord with its root in the bass. Learn more: Root Motion
Rotation
The same loop of chords started from a different point. vi – IV – I – V is a rotation of I – V – vi – IV. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related

S

Scale
A set of notes arranged in order of pitch, which a key or melody is built from. Major, minor, pentatonic and the modes are all scales. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Secondary dominant
A dominant chord aimed at a chord other than the tonic. In C, D7 is the "V of V": it pulls to G. Written V/V, or II7. Learn more: What Each Chord Does · How Chord Progressions Are Related
Semitone
Another name for a half step.
Sequence
A musical pattern repeated at a different pitch, such as a chain of chords falling by fifths. Learn more: Root Motion
Seventh chord
A triad with one more note stacked on top, a 7th above the root. The main types are major 7th, dominant 7th, minor 7th, half-diminished and diminished 7th.
Sharp
The sign ♯, which raises a note by a half step. F♯ is a half step above F.
Shuffle
A bouncing rhythmic feel in which each beat is split long-short. The classic feel of the blues.
Slash chord
A chord written with a specific bass note after a slash: C/E means a C chord with E in the bass. Learn more: Root Motion
Subdominant
The 4th degree of a scale, and the chord built on it: IV (F in C). The "away" function, which leads from home toward tension. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Submediant
The 6th degree of a scale, and the chord built on it: vi (Am in C). It often stands in for the tonic. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Substitution
Replacing a chord with another that does a similar job, usually because they share notes. Am can substitute for C, and Dm for F. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Subtonic
The 7th degree of the natural minor scale, a whole step below the home note, and the chord built on it: VII (G in A minor). It leads home more gently than the dominant. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Supertonic
The 2nd degree of a scale, and the chord built on it: ii (Dm in C). Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Suspended chord
A chord in which the 3rd is replaced by the 2nd (sus2: C – D – G) or the 4th (sus4: C – F – G). Neither major nor minor, it sounds open and waiting to resolve.
Suspension
A note held over from one chord into the next, clashing briefly before it steps down to a chord tone (a 4 – 3 suspension). Learn more: Cadences
Swing
A rhythmic feel, central to jazz, in which pairs of eighth notes are played long-short.

T

Tempo
The speed of the beat, usually given in beats per minute (BPM).
Third
The distance from one note to the note three letter names above it. A major third is four half steps (C – E); a minor third is three (C – E♭). The third decides whether a chord is major or minor. Learn more: Root Motion
Time signature
The two numbers at the start of a piece that show how many beats are in a bar, and which note value gets the beat. 4/4 means four quarter-note beats per bar.
Tonic
The home note of a key, and the chord built on it: I (C in C major). It sounds settled and at rest. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Tonnetz
A grid of notes, first drawn by Leonhard Euler in 1739, with fifths along one direction and thirds along the others. Triads form triangles, so related chords sit side by side. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Transposition
Moving a piece of music to a different key, keeping all its relationships the same. Roman numerals make transposing easy. Learn more: The Circle of Fifths
Triad
A three-note chord built from a root, a 3rd and a 5th. There are four kinds: major, minor, diminished and augmented. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Tritone
The interval of three whole steps (six half steps), such as B – F. It divides the octave exactly in half and is the most unstable interval, the tension at the heart of the dominant 7th chord. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
Tritone substitution
Replacing a dominant 7th chord with the dominant 7th a tritone away: D♭7 instead of G7 before C. Both contain the same tritone (F and B/C♭), so the resolution still works, with a chromatic bass. Learn more: Root Motion · The Circle of Fifths
Tuning
The notes a guitar's open strings are set to. Standard tuning is E A D G B E, low to high.
Turnaround
A short chord pattern at the end of a section that leads back to the beginning, such as the last two bars of the 12-bar blues, or I – vi – ii – V. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related
Twelve-bar blues
The most common blues form: twelve bars of I7, IV7 and V7, arranged as four bars of I7, two of IV7, two of I7, then V7 – IV7 – I7 – V7. Learn more: How Chord Progressions Are Related

U

Unison
Two or more parts playing exactly the same note.

V

Voice leading
The way the individual notes of each chord move to the notes of the next. Good voice leading keeps common tones still and moves the rest by small steps. Learn more: Root Motion
Voicing
The way the notes of a chord are arranged: which octave each note is in, which is on top, and which is doubled. The same chord can be voiced many ways on piano or guitar.

W

Whole step
Two half steps: C to D, or two frets on a guitar. Also called a whole tone. Learn more: What Each Chord Does
