Scales and Modes

A scale is a set of notes played in order, step by step, from one note up to the same note an octave higher. Scales are the raw material of music: melodies are made from the notes of a scale, and so are chords. When you know which scale a song uses, you know which notes belong in it and which will sound out of place. This page starts with the most important scale of all, the major scale, and builds up to the minor scales, the pentatonic and blues scales, the seven modes, and a technique jazz players use to make a melody line spell out the chord underneath it.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. The scale itself is the first thing we'll look at.

Part 1: The basics

What a scale is

If you look at the white keys on a piano, starting from any C and playing every white key up to the next C, here's what you'll find: eight notes, C D E F G A B. Those notes, in that order, are the C major scale.

Every scale has a home note, the one it starts and ends on. That note is called the tonic, and the scale is named after it, so the tonic of the C major scale is C. Each note in the scale also gets a number, called its degree, counting up from the tonic: C is degree 1, D is degree 2, E is degree 3, and so on up to B at degree 7. (The C at the top is degree 1 again, an octave higher.) Musicians use these numbers all the time, because they work in any key: "degree 5" means the fifth note of whatever scale you're in.

The pattern of steps

Here's the most important thing to understand about any scale: what makes it a major scale isn't which notes it uses, but the distances between them. Going up the C major scale, the gap from each note to the next follows this pattern:

whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half

A half step is the distance from one key to the very next key (white or black), or from one fret to the next fret on the same string. A whole step is two half steps. In C major, the two half steps fall between E and F, and between B and C, the only two pairs of white keys with no black key between them.

That pattern is the recipe. Start on any note at all, follow whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half, and the notes you land on are that note's major scale. That's why every major scale sounds alike: they all have the same shape, just starting in different places.

The note line below shows that shape. If the note line is new to you: each column is one key on the piano (or one fret on the guitar), the shaded columns are black keys, and each coloured dot is a note of the scale. Count the columns between the dots and you'll see the pattern: two columns (a whole step) everywhere, except E to F and B to C, which are one column (a half step).

Written on a staff, the same scale climbs one step at a time: from a line to the space above it, then to the next line, and so on. Each note sits one letter name above the last. One thing the staff doesn't show is the difference between a whole step and a half step: E to F looks exactly the same as C to D. That's why the note line is useful alongside it.

Reading the fretboard as a scale

In Warbling Wren, the guitar neck always shows the scale of the key you've chosen, behind whatever chord you're playing. Here's how to read it. Every shaded dot on the neck is a note of the scale. The shade tells you the degree: degree 1, the tonic, is the darkest and has a ring around it, and the shades get lighter for each degree after it, up to degree 7. Play the dots in order along one string, or work across the strings within a few frets, and you're playing the scale.

This matters because the chords of a key are built from the same notes as its scale. Watch the neck as you change chords below: the chord's dots move, but the scale behind them stays put, because all three chords come from the same scale.

The minor scale

If you take the same white keys but start and stop on A instead of C, here's what you'll find: A B C D E F G A. Every note is the same as in C major, but the home note has moved to A, and that changes the sound completely. This is the A natural minor scale, and it sounds darker and sadder than C major.

Why does the same set of notes sound so different? Because the pattern of steps now starts in a different place. Counting up from A, the steps are: whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole. The half step now comes second instead of third, so the third note of the scale (C) is only a minor third above the tonic. That small third is what makes minor sound minor.

Two keys that share all their notes like this, one major and one minor, are called relative keys: A minor is the relative minor of C major. On the circle of fifths they sit in the same spot, the major key on the outside ring and its relative minor just inside it.

Part 2: Going deeper

Three kinds of minor

The natural minor scale has one weakness. In a major scale, the seventh note sits just a half step below the tonic (B is right below C in C major), and that closeness pulls the ear strongly back home. In the A natural minor scale, the seventh note is G, a whole step below A. With that bigger gap, the pull home is much weaker, and minor-key songs can sound like they never quite arrive.

Musicians solved this in two ways, and each gives a new version of the minor scale:

  • Harmonic minor raises the seventh note by a half step, from G to G♯. Now the seventh is right below the tonic, just like in a major scale, and it pulls hard into A. It also turns the chord on the fifth degree from minor (Em) into major (E), which gives minor keys a much stronger "come home" chord.
  • Melodic minor raises the sixth note as well, from F to F♯. Raising the seventh alone leaves an awkward, wide gap between F and G♯ (three half steps). Raising the sixth too smooths that out, so the scale flows more easily in a melody.

The note line stacks all three so you can see exactly which notes change. Read down each column: everything stays put except the sixth and seventh dots, which move one column to the right.

Meet the chord: E7

E7 is the V7 chord of A harmonic minor: E–G♯–B–D. It's built on degree 5 of the scale, and it contains the raised seventh, G♯. That means it's the chord that does the work harmonic minor was invented for: its G♯ sits a half step below A, so when E7 moves to Am, that note steps up and the music lands firmly home.

Pentatonic scales

A pentatonic scale is a scale with five notes instead of seven ("penta" means five). If you start from the major scale and leave out two of its notes, the fourth and the seventh, here's what you get: the major pentatonic scale. In C, that's C D E G A.

Those two missing notes are exactly the ones that sit a half step from a neighbour (F next to E, and B next to C). With them gone, there are no half steps left anywhere in the scale, and that's the pentatonic's secret: no two notes rub against each other, so almost any note sounds good over almost any chord in the key. That's why it's the first scale many guitarists learn to improvise with.

Start those same five notes on A instead of C, and you have the A minor pentatonic scale: A C D E G. It's the relative minor of the major pentatonic, the same notes with a different home, and it's the most played scale in rock and blues guitar.

The blues scale

If you add one note to the minor pentatonic, the flattened fifth, here's what you get: the blues scale. In A, the extra note is E♭, and the full scale is A C D E♭ E G.

That extra note is called the blue note. It isn't meant to be held on its own; it's a passing note that slides between D and E. On its way through, it creates a brief, deliberate clash, and that gritty, crying sound is what makes a line sound like the blues.

  • That's: the blues scale: the minor pentatonic with one blue note added.

The seven modes

You've now seen that the white keys make C major when you start on C, and A minor when you start on A. That raises a question: what happens if you start on one of the other white keys? Here's what you'll find: every starting note gives a different scale with its own mood, even though the notes never change. These seven scales are called the modes.

The reason each one sounds different is the same reason major and minor do: the pattern of whole steps and half steps starts in a different place, so the distances from the home note change. Here are the seven, from the brightest-sounding to the darkest, each with the white key it starts on:

  • Lydian (start on F): the brightest. Like major, but even more open and dreamy, almost floating.
  • Ionian (start on C): the ordinary major scale.
  • Mixolydian (start on G): major, but relaxed and bluesy.
  • Dorian (start on D): minor, but with a hopeful lift. Common in funk, folk and jazz.
  • Aeolian (start on A): the ordinary natural minor scale.
  • Phrygian (start on E): minor and dark, with a Spanish flavour.
  • Locrian (start on B): the darkest and least stable. It's rarely used as a home.

The circle of fifths shows why they line up this way. The seven white-key notes sit side by side on the circle, from F to B. Each step clockwise starts the mode on a note one fifth higher, and each step makes the scale a little darker, because one more of its notes ends up lower relative to the home note.

Part 3: For the professional

This part covers how to recognise a mode by ear, how scales and chords fit together when improvising, and enclosures, a way of building melody lines that land on the chord.

The chord that gives a mode away

On paper, the modes can seem interchangeable, because they all use the same seven notes. So how do you tell, just by listening, whether a song is in D Dorian or plain D minor? The answer is to listen for the one note that makes the mode different.

Each mode can be compared with the scale it's closest to: the major-sounding modes with the major scale, the minor-sounding modes with the natural minor scale. When you compare them, each mode turns out to differ from its neighbour by just one note. That one note is the mode's fingerprint, called its characteristic note.

Melodies can avoid a single note, but chords are harder to hide. In each mode, the characteristic note shows up inside one particular chord, and that chord appears in no ordinary major or minor key built on the same home note. So when you hear that chord sitting next to the home chord, you've found the mode. Here are the four modes where this is easiest to hear:

Once you know the four fingerprints, you can use them in your own playing: to make a song sound Lydian, Mixolydian, Dorian or Phrygian, put the giveaway chord next to the home chord, and keep coming back home.

Chords and scales together

When improvising, players often think of each chord as having its own scale, called its chord-scale. In the key of C major, for example, the scale for the Dm7 chord is D Dorian, and the scale for the G7 chord is G Mixolydian. They're the same seven notes as C major, but thinking of the scale from the chord's own root tells you which notes are "safe" to land on (the chord's own notes) and which add colour.

This connects directly to arpeggios. An arpeggio plays only a chord's own notes. A scale adds the notes that fall between them. Improvisers move between the two: arpeggio notes when they want to sound exactly like the chord, scale notes to travel smoothly from one chord note to the next.

Enclosures: surrounding a chord note

An enclosure is a technique from jazz and bebop for making a melody line sound like it's aiming at the chord. Here's how it works. First, pick a note of the chord you want to land on; this is the target note. Then, before you play it, play two notes that surround it: the note just above it in the scale, then the note a half step below it. Finally, land on the target.

Those two surrounding notes are called approach notes. Because they sit on either side of the target, they "enclose" it, and the ear hears the target as the goal the line was heading for all along.

The staff below shows two enclosures over a C chord. Read it left to right, one note per bar. The labels underneath say which job each note does: above, below, then the target. The first enclosure targets C; the second targets E.

Look at the second enclosure. The note below the target is D♯, and D♯ isn't in the C major scale. That's normal: the approach from below is usually exactly a half step under the target, even when that means borrowing a note from outside the scale. The clash lasts only an instant, and it makes the arrival on the target sound even more satisfying.

Try it Enclose every note of the C chord

  • What you'll hear: three enclosures over a C chord: D, B, C; then F, D♯, E; then A, F♯, G. Each group ends on a note of the chord.
  • Listen for: each target note sounding like an arrival, as if the line were circling it before touching down.
  • That's: enclosures: the classic way to make a melody line spell out the chord underneath it.

There are many variations on the basic pattern: approaching from below first and then above, approaching with two half steps from below (called a double chromatic approach), or chaining enclosures together. They all do the same job: make the target note sound inevitable.

Other scales you'll meet

Every scale has its own set of modes, not just the major scale. The one musicians usually meet first comes from harmonic minor. If you take the A harmonic minor scale and start it on E, here's what you get: E F G♯ A B C D E. This is called Phrygian dominant. It's like the Phrygian mode, with its tense half step above home, but with a major third (G♯), so it sounds dark and bright at the same time. You'll hear it in flamenco, Middle Eastern music and heavy metal.

Why the major scale works so well

If you find the seven notes of C major on the circle of fifths, here's what you'll see: they sit side by side, in one unbroken run, F → C → G → D → A → E → B. That means the scale's notes are spread out as evenly as seven notes out of twelve can be, a property called being maximally even.

That evenness explains two things. First, it's why the major scale contains so many good-sounding chords: its notes are spread out enough to stack into clean thirds on almost every degree. Second, it's why changing key one step around the circle sounds so smooth: moving the run along by one step swaps out just one note. The Circle of Fifths page goes into this in more detail.

Using it at the instrument

  • Say the step pattern out loud as you play: "whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half". Then build a major scale starting from G and from F, and check each note against the pattern.
  • On guitar, learn one scale shape in one position first, within four or five frets. Then use the app's shaded neck to find the same notes in the next position up the fretboard.
  • Play the minor pentatonic over a slow blues, and add the blue note only when you want grit.
  • Hear the modes over a drone. Hold a low D and play the white keys from D up to the next D; then hold a low E and play from E up to the next E. Same notes, two different moods.
  • Practise enclosures slowly, on one target note at a time, then on every note of a chord.
  • Combine arpeggios and scales: play a chord's arpeggio going up, then come back down using the scale.
