Scales and Modes

A scale is a set of notes in order, from one note up to the same note an octave higher. Melodies are made from scales, and so are chords. Once you know a scale, you know which notes "belong" in a song. This page starts with the most important scale of all and works up to the seven modes and a trick jazz players use to make a line sound like the chord.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. The scale itself is the first thing we'll look at.

How to read the diagrams: on the keyboard, the coloured keys are the chord's notes. On the guitar, each dot is a finger on a fret, an open circle above the nut is an open string, and × means don't play that string. The darkest dot is the chord's root, the note it's named after. A ✦ tag under a diagram marks a quirky shape: a guitar shape on three strings, up the neck or without its 5th, or a keyboard drawing inverted to fit one octave. Quirky isn't wrong. Like the knots in wood, these voicings have a character of their own.

Part 1: The basics

What a scale is

Play the white keys on a piano from C up to the next C: C D E F G A B. That's the C major scale. The first note, C, is home base. It's called the tonic, and the scale is named after it. Each note in the scale has a number, its degree: C is 1, D is 2, and so on up to B at 7.

C major
C1D2E3F4G5A6B7

The pattern of steps

What makes a major scale major isn't which notes it uses, but the gaps between them. Going up, the steps are: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. (A half step is the very next key or fret; a whole step is two.) Start on any note, follow that pattern, and you've built a major scale.

On the staff, the scale climbs one line or space at a time: one letter name each.

Reading the fretboard as a scale

In Warbling Wren, the guitar neck always shows the scale of the key you're in. Every dot is a note of the scale, shaded by its degree: degree 1 (the tonic) is darkest, with a ring around it, and the shades get lighter up to degree 7. Follow the dots along one string, or across the strings, and you're playing the scale.

The minor scale

Play the same white keys, but start and end on A: A B C D E F G A. Now the home note is A, and the pattern of steps has moved: whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole. That's the A natural minor scale. Same notes as C major, different home, darker sound. Keys that share their notes like this are called relatives.

Part 2: Going deeper

Three kinds of minor

The natural minor scale has one weakness: its 7th note (G in A minor) is a whole step below home, so it doesn't pull home very strongly. Musicians fixed that two ways:

  • Harmonic minor raises the 7th by a half step, to G♯. Now there's a strong pull up into A, and the V chord becomes major (E instead of Em).
  • Melodic minor raises the 6th and the 7th (F♯ and G♯), which smooths out the big gap harmonic minor leaves between F and G♯.

Meet the chord: E7

E7 is the V7 chord of A harmonic minor: E–G♯–B–D. Its G♯ is the raised 7th, a half step below A, which is why it pulls so hard into Am.

Pentatonic scales

Take the major scale and leave out its 4th and 7th, and you get five notes: the major pentatonic ("penta" means five). With no half steps left, every note sounds good with every other. Start the same five notes on A and you have the minor pentatonic, the most played scale in rock and blues guitar.

A minor pentatonic
A1C♭3D4E5G♭7

The blues scale

Add one note to the minor pentatonic, the flat 5th (E♭ in A), and you get the blues scale. That extra note is a passing note: it slides between D and E and gives the blues its gritty, crying sound.

The seven modes

You've seen that the white keys make C major starting on C, and A minor starting on A. Start on any of the other white keys and you get a different scale, with a different mood. These seven scales are called the modes. On the circle of fifths, they line up from brightest to darkest.

  • Lydian (start on F): the brightest, dreamy and floating.
  • Ionian (start on C): the major scale itself.
  • Mixolydian (start on G): major, but bluesy and relaxed.
  • Dorian (start on D): minor, but hopeful. Common in funk and folk.
  • Aeolian (start on A): the natural minor scale.
  • Phrygian (start on E): minor and dark, with a Spanish flavour.
  • Locrian (start on B): the darkest, unstable and rarely used as home.

Chords and scales together

Every chord has a scale that fits it, called its chord-scale. Over Dm7 in C major, D Dorian fits; over G7, G Mixolydian. They're the same seven notes, but thinking of the scale from the chord's root tells you which notes are "safe" (the chord's own notes) and which add colour.

An arpeggio plays only a chord's notes. A scale adds the notes between them. Improvisers move between the two: arpeggio notes to sound like the chord, scale notes to connect them.

Enclosures: surrounding a chord note

Here's a trick from jazz and bebop. Pick a chord note you want to land on, the target note. Before you play it, play the scale note just above it and the note a half step below it. Then land on the target. Those two notes "enclose" it, and the ear hears the target as the goal of the line. This is called an enclosure, and the notes before the target are approach notes.

Notice D♯: it isn't in C major. Approach notes from below are often a half step away even when that note is outside the scale. The clash is brief, and it makes the target sound even more like home.

Try it Enclose every note of C

  • What you'll hear: over a C chord: D, B, C, then F, D♯, E, then A, F♯, G.
  • Listen for: each target note sounding like an arrival.
  • That's: enclosures: the classic way to make a line spell out the chord.

There are variations: below then above, two half steps from below (a double chromatic approach), or a whole chain of them. They all do the same job: make the target note sound inevitable.

Other scales players meet

Every scale has its own set of modes. The one players meet first comes from harmonic minor: start A harmonic minor on E and you get Phrygian dominant (E F G♯ A B C D E), a major-sounding Phrygian heard in flamenco, Middle Eastern music and metal.

Why the major scale works so well

On the circle of fifths, the seven notes of C major sit side by side: F → C → G → D → A → E → B. That makes them as spread out as seven notes out of twelve can be, a property called being maximally even. It's part of why the major scale has so many good-sounding chords, and why moving one note at a time around the circle changes key so smoothly. The Circle of Fifths page goes further.

Using it at the instrument

  • Say the step pattern out loud as you play: "whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half". Then build a major scale from G and from F.
  • On guitar, learn one scale shape in one position first. Then use the app's shaded neck to find the same notes in the next position up.
  • Play the minor pentatonic over a slow blues and add the blue note only when you want grit.
  • Hear the modes by drone. Hold a low D and play the white keys from D to D; then hold E and play from E to E. Same notes, two moods.
  • Practise enclosures slowly on one target note at a time, then on every note of a chord.
  • Mix arpeggio and scale: play a chord's arpeggio up, then come down the scale.
