Intervals: The Distance Between Two Notes

An interval is the distance from one note to another. That's all it is. But every chord, every scale and every chord progression on this site is built out of intervals, so once you can see them, everything else starts to make sense. This page starts from nothing. You don't need to read music or know any theory.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. On this page we'll mostly use single notes from these scales, two at a time.

How to read the diagrams: on the keyboard, the coloured keys are the chord's notes. On the guitar, each dot is a finger on a fret, an open circle above the nut is an open string, and × means don't play that string. The darkest dot is the chord's root, the note it's named after. A ✦ tag under a diagram marks a quirky shape: a guitar shape on three strings, up the neck or without its 5th, or a keyboard drawing inverted to fit one octave. Quirky isn't wrong. Like the knots in wood, these voicings have a character of their own.

Part 1: The basics

Straighten out the keyboard

On a piano, the white keys and black keys are different shapes, which makes it hard to see how far apart two notes really are. So let's straighten the keyboard out. In the picture below, every key, white or black, gets one column of the same width. The shaded columns are the black keys. Each column is one step up from the one before it.

On a guitar, it works the same way: each column is one fret. Move one fret up the fretboard on the same string (toward the guitar's body, from fret 2 to fret 3, say) and you move one column to the right. Move one fret down the fretboard (toward the headstock) and you move one column to the left.

Half steps and whole steps

The smallest move in this kind of music is one column: the very next key on the piano, or the very next fret on the guitar. That move is called a half step. Two half steps together make a whole step.

Look at E to F, and B to C. There's no black key between them, so they are only a half step apart, even though they are both white keys. Everywhere else, white keys are a whole step apart, with a black key in between.

Counting the distance

Every interval has a size and a name. The size is how many half steps (columns) you move. The name comes from counting letter names, and you count the note you start on as 1. From C up to E: C is 1, D is 2, E is 3. So C up to E is a third.

Why both? Because the letter count alone isn't exact. C up to E and A up to C are both thirds (three letters), but one is 4 half steps and the other is 3. You can see the difference in the stacked lines below: the bracket shows the size, and the dots show where each note sits.

The intervals from C

Here are the most useful intervals, all starting from C. Each row moves one note further away. Notice how the gap grows one column at a time, and how the last one lands on another C: twelve half steps up, the same note again, just higher. That's an octave.

Try it Three intervals you already know

You've heard these intervals thousands of times without knowing their names. All three start on the same note, C, so the only thing that changes from card to card is the top note. Play each pair and listen to how different they feel.

octave
12 half steps · P8
C → C
  • What you'll hear: C and the C 12 half steps above it: an octave. On the keyboard, the two keys are the same letter, seven white keys apart. On the guitar, C on the 5th string at fret 3 and C on the 2nd string at fret 1.
  • Listen for: how the two notes melt into one. It sounds like the same note played twice, one higher. Men and women singing "the same" tune are usually singing in octaves.
perfect 5th
7 half steps · P5
C → G
  • What you'll hear: C and G, 7 half steps apart: a perfect fifth. On the guitar, C on the 5th string at fret 3 and G on the open 3rd string.
  • Listen for: an open, hollow, powerful sound. The two notes are clearly different, but they lock together. This is the rock power chord: no sweetness, no sadness, just strength.
major 3rd
4 half steps · M3
C → E
  • What you'll hear: C and E, 4 half steps apart: a major third. On the guitar, C on the 5th string at fret 3 and E on the 4th string at fret 2.
  • Listen for: warmth and brightness. Of the three, this is the first that sounds like it has a mood: happy and sweet. Add it to the fifth and you'd have a full major chord.
  • That's: the three intervals hidden inside almost every chord. The octave doubles a note, the fifth gives a chord its strength, and the third gives it its mood. A C chord is just C with a third (E) and a fifth (G) above it.

Where chords fall on the line

Intervals don't just describe two notes. They're also how chords are built, and how chords move. A chord is a few intervals stacked up. The C chord is C, E and G: the root (C), a third above it (E), then another third on top (G). The root is the note the chord is named after.

A chord progression is a series of chords played one after another. Below, four chords, C → F → G → C, are stacked on the same note line, one row each, in the order they're played. Each row shows that chord's three notes. The dotted line joins the roots, so you can follow how far the music moves at each change.

Count the columns along the dotted line and every chord change turns out to be an interval:

  • C to F: the root moves up 5 half steps, a perfect fourth.
  • F to G: the root moves up 2 half steps, a whole step.
  • G back to C: the root moves down 7 half steps, a perfect fifth.

So what does that give you? Three things:

  • It explains the sound. How a progression feels depends on how far its roots move. Fourths and fifths, the moves in this one, are the strongest and most settled moves in music, and that's why C → F → G → C sounds so solid that it's used in thousands of blues, rock and country songs. Moves by a step, like F to G, sound bolder. The Root Motion page explores this in depth.
  • It shows which notes stay put. Look straight down the columns. G is in the C chord and the G chord, in the same column, so it doesn't move at all when you change from one to the other. On the piano you can keep that finger down; on the guitar it often means one string stays the same. Notes that two chords share are what make a chord change sound smooth.
  • It works in every key. The progression isn't really "C, F, G". It's "up a fourth, up a whole step, down a fifth". Start those same moves from any other root and you get the same progression, sounding the same way, in a new key. That's why musicians learn progressions as intervals (and as Roman numerals, I, IV and V, which name the same distances) instead of as letters.

Part 2: Going deeper

Major, minor and perfect

Two intervals with the same letter count can be different sizes, as the thirds showed. So musicians add a word in front. Seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths come in two sizes: the bigger one is major and the smaller one, a half step less, is minor. A major third is 4 half steps; a minor third is 3.

Fourths, fifths and octaves are different. They come in one main size, and they sound so stable and pure that they're called perfect. A perfect fifth is 7 half steps, and it's one step around the circle of fifths: C to G, G to D, all the way around.

Try it Major and minor thirds

Here are two intervals with the same name, "third", but different sizes. Play one, then the other, and listen for how one half step changes the mood.

major 3rd
4 half steps · M3
C → E
  • What you'll hear: C up to E: a major third. Count the keys on the keyboard, white and black: 4 half steps. On the guitar, C on the 5th string at fret 3, then E on the next thinner string (the 4th) at fret 2, one fret down the fretboard.
  • Listen for: a bright, open, happy sound. This is the third at the bottom of every major chord.
minor 3rd
3 half steps · m3
A → C
  • What you'll hear: A up to C: a minor third. Count the keys again: 3 half steps, one fewer than the major third. On the guitar, A on the 6th string at fret 5, then C on the next thinner string (the 5th) at fret 3, two frets down the fretboard.
  • Listen for: a darker, softer, slightly sad sound. Compare it with the card above: only one half step smaller, but a completely different mood. This is the third at the bottom of every minor chord.
  • That's: major and minor, the most important difference in harmony. Both are "thirds" because both span three letter names (C–D–E and A–B–C), but one is 4 half steps and the other is 3. That single half step is what makes a chord sound happy or sad. You'll see this again on the How Chords Are Built page.

The tritone

Right in the middle of the octave, 6 half steps from the bottom, is an interval with a big personality: the tritone (three whole steps). It sounds restless, as if it can't wait to move somewhere else. In C major, it lives between B and F, and those two notes are inside the G7 chord. When B moves up to C and F moves down to E, the tension lets go.

Interval shapes on the guitar

On the guitar, every interval has a shape: a pattern of two dots that you can slide anywhere on the fretboard and that always makes the same interval. (The strings are numbered from the thinnest, the 1st, to the thickest, the 6th. "Up the fretboard" means toward the guitar's body, to higher fret numbers and higher notes; "down the fretboard" means toward the headstock.)

The perfect fifth shape is: put your finger on the root, then cross to the next thinner string and move two frets up the fretboard. The drawing below shows it three times, starting from three different roots. The roots are the dots with a dark ring; the fifths have no ring:

  • Root G on the 6th string at fret 3. Its fifth, D, is on the 5th string at fret 5.
  • Root C on the 5th string at fret 3. Its fifth, G, is on the 4th string at fret 5.
  • Root D on the open 4th string (fret 0). Its fifth, A, is on the 3rd string at fret 2.

Each time, the fifth is one string thinner and two frets higher than its root: fret 3 to fret 5, or open (fret 0) to fret 2. Learn the shape once and you can play a fifth from any note on those strings.

There's one catch. When a shape crosses from the 3rd string (G) to the 2nd string (B), you have to move one extra fret up the fretboard, because those two strings are tuned closer together than the others. So a fifth from A on the 3rd string at fret 2 lands on E on the 2nd string at fret 5: three frets up, not two.

Turning an interval upside down

Take C up to G: a fifth. Now keep the G and move the C up an octave, so G is on the bottom: G up to C is a fourth. Flipping an interval like this is called inverting it, and the two numbers always add up to 9: a fifth flips to a fourth, a third to a sixth, a second to a seventh.

Try it A fifth and its fourth

Inverting an interval means moving its bottom note up an octave so it ends up on top. Here's a fifth, and the fourth it turns into.

perfect 5th
7 half steps · P5
C → G
  • What you'll hear: C on the bottom and G above it: a perfect fifth, 7 half steps. On the guitar, C on the 5th string at fret 3 and G on the open 3rd string.
  • Listen for: a wide, strong, settled sound, with C as the clear foundation.
perfect 4th
5 half steps · P4
G → C
  • What you'll hear: the same two note names flipped: G on the bottom and C above it, a perfect fourth, 5 half steps. On the guitar, G on the 6th string at fret 3, and C straight across on the next thinner string (the 5th) at the same fret, fret 3.
  • Listen for: a tighter, slightly unsettled sound, almost like a question. It's still open and clean, because it's the same two notes, but now C feels like it's on top, waiting to be answered.
  • That's: an inverted pair. The fifth (7 half steps) and the fourth (5 half steps) add up to 12, exactly one octave, and their names always add up to 9 (5 + 4). That's why, on the Root Motion page, moving "up a fourth" and moving "down a fifth" land on the same note name: they're two directions to the same place.

Intervals on the staff

You don't need to read music to use this site, but here's one thing written music shows nicely. Notes sit on the lines and in the spaces, one letter name each. A third always sits on two lines next to each other (or two spaces). A fifth skips one more line. So you can see an interval's letter count at a glance.

Part 3: For the professional

These ideas are where intervals meet harmony and sound itself. Every term is explained as it comes up.

Consonance and dissonance

A consonant interval sounds settled: octaves, fifths, fourths, thirds and sixths. A dissonant one sounds tense and wants to move: seconds, sevenths and the tritone. The reason is physics. Every note we hear is really a stack of quieter, higher notes called overtones. When two notes share many overtones, as an octave or a fifth does, they blend. When they share few, the overtones rub against each other, and we hear that rubbing as tension.

Dissonance isn't bad. It's what makes music move: tension, then release.

Compound intervals

An interval bigger than an octave is called compound. A ninth is a second plus an octave (C up to the D above the next C). An eleventh is a fourth plus an octave, and a thirteenth is a sixth plus an octave. They have the same letter as the smaller interval, but spread out, so they sound more open. Jazz chords use them all the time.

Meet the chord: C9

major 2nd
2 half steps · M2
C → D
  • What you'll hear: C and the D right next door: a major second, 2 half steps. On the keyboard, two neighbouring white keys. On the guitar, C on the 5th string at fret 3 and D on the open 4th string.
  • Listen for: a rub. The two notes are so close that they buzz against each other. It's not ugly, but it's busy and tense.
major 9th
14 half steps · M9
C → D
  • What you'll hear: the same two letters, but with D an octave higher: a major ninth, 14 half steps. The keyboard stretches to two octaves to fit it. On the guitar, C on the 5th string at fret 3 and D on the 2nd string at fret 3, three strings thinner at the same fret.
  • Listen for: the rub disappearing. Spread apart, C and D sound open, airy and modern instead of tense.
  • That's: a compound interval: an interval wider than an octave. Spreading the same two notes further apart gives them room, so they stop clashing and start to blend. That's why jazz and pop chords often put the 9th high above the rest of the chord: all the colour of the second, without the rub.

Same sound, different name

On a piano, G♯ and A♭ are the same key. But in music they're different notes, because they have different letter names. C up to A♭ is a minor sixth (six letters); C up to G♯ is an augmented fifth (five letters). Notes like this, the same key with two names, are called enharmonic. The name tells you which way the note wants to go: a G♯ usually wants to rise to A, a A♭ usually wants to fall to G.

Meet the chords: C+ and A♭

C+ (say "C augmented") is C–E–G♯, with its top note raised to G♯. A♭ is A♭–C–E♭. Neither is in C major: C+ comes from a scale called harmonic minor, and A♭ is borrowed from C minor.

Using it at the instrument

  • Sing before you play. Pick an interval from the song table, sing it, then find it on your instrument. Your ear learns faster when your voice leads.
  • Count columns, not keys. On the piano, count every key, white and black, to measure an interval in half steps. On the guitar, count frets.
  • Learn the fifth shape on guitar first: root on the 6th or 5th string, then the next thinner string, two frets up the fretboard. It's the power chord, and it's the most useful shape on the guitar.
  • Play every interval from the same note. Start on C and play each interval in the grid above, one after another, until you can hear each one's character.
  • Listen for thirds in chords. Play C and pick out the two thirds inside it (C to E, then E to G).
  • In Warbling Wren, tap the tone circles one at a time and name the interval each note makes with the root.
