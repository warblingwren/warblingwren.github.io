Reading the Fretboard

The guitar neck is a grid: six strings running one way, frets running the other. Every place your finger can land is a note, and most notes appear in more than one place. This page shows you how to find any note, how to read the chord diagrams on this site, and how the same chord can be played all the way up the neck.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). If you find these chords on your piano or guitar now, every example further down will already be under your fingers. The guitar diagrams under each chord are the ones this page teaches you to read.

How to read the diagrams: on the keyboard, the coloured keys are the chord's notes. On the guitar, each dot is a finger on a fret, an open circle above the nut is an open string, and × means don't play that string. The darkest dot is the chord's root, the note it's named after. A ✦ tag under a diagram marks a quirky shape: a guitar shape on three strings, up the neck or without its 5th, or a keyboard drawing inverted to fit one octave. Quirky isn't wrong. Like the knots in wood, these voicings have a character of their own.

Part 1: The basics

Strings and frets

The six strings are numbered from the thinnest (1st) to the thickest (6th). In standard tuning, played open (with no fingers down), they sound E A D G B E from thickest to thinnest. A popular way to remember them: Every Amateur Does Get Better Eventually.

The metal strips across the neck are frets. Pressing a string just behind a fret raises the note by one half step for every fret. Fret 12 is an octave above the open string: the same note again, higher.

Finding notes on the two low strings

Most chord shapes start on the 6th or the 5th string, so those two are the ones to learn first. To find any note on them, count up from the open note, one half step per fret, remembering that E to F and B to C are only one fret apart (there's no sharp or flat between them).

Reading a chord diagram

Every chord on this site has a guitar diagram. If you picture the neck standing upright and facing you, the diagram makes sense at once: the vertical lines are the strings (6th on the left), the horizontal lines are frets, and the thick bar at the top is the nut.

Part 2: Going deeper

Octave shapes

The same note shows up in several places on the neck. From any note on the 6th or 5th string, go two strings up and two frets along and you land on the same note an octave higher. (Crossing the 2nd string, add one more fret.) These octave shapes let you find any note quickly.

One chord, five places: the CAGED system

Most guitarists learn their first chords in the open position, near the nut, and then wonder how anyone plays higher up the neck. The answer is that the five open chord shapes C, A, G, E and D can each slide up the neck, and any of them can play any chord. If you play one chord in all five shapes, one after another, they link up end to end and cover the whole neck. Guitar teachers call this the CAGED system, after the order the shapes come in.

The map below shows every C, E and G on the neck, the three notes of the C chord, with the stretch each shape covers shaded and labelled above it. Notice that the shaded stretches overlap: each shape shares a few notes with the next one, which is how the shapes lock together into one continuous map.

Here are the six shapes one at a time. In each card, the dots are the notes you play, the grey bar is a barre (one finger laid flat across several strings), and the text tells you where the root is, which finger does what, and how the shape hands over to the next one.

1 The C shape, open position

The open C chord you probably already know. The root is on the 5th string at fret 3, under the ring finger, and the top strings ring open. Its highest notes, at frets 0 to 3, are where the next shape begins.

2 The A shape, barre at fret 3

The open A chord moved up three frets, with the index finger as a barre where the nut used to be. The root is on the 5th string at fret 3, the same note the C shape used. The three notes at fret 5 are played by the ring finger, flattened across them.

3 The G shape, frets 5 to 8

The open G chord moved up five frets. The root is on the 6th string at fret 8 and again on the 3rd string at fret 5. It's the stretchiest shape, so players often use just part of it, such as the top four strings.

4 The E shape, barre at fret 8

The open E chord moved up eight frets, with the index finger barring all six strings. The root is on the 6th string at fret 8, the same note that ended the G shape. This is the most-used barre shape of all.

5 The D shape, frets 10 to 13

The open D chord moved up ten frets, played on the top four strings only. The root is on the 4th string at fret 10, an octave above the E shape's root on the 6th string.

6 Back to the C shape, an octave up

Fret 12 is the nut again, an octave higher, so the C shape returns with the index finger playing the old open strings. From here, the whole cycle repeats.

The order never changes: C, A, G, E, D, then C again. That means if you know where one shape is, you know where the next one starts, for any chord. For G, the cycle starts with the G shape in the open position; for D, it starts with the D shape. Try finding all five shapes of A by starting from the open A chord and working up.

Meet the chords: A, E, D

A, E and D are three of the five CAGED shapes. They're major chords from outside C major: A has C♯, E has G♯, and D has F♯.

Movable barre chords

A barre chord replaces the nut with your index finger. Lay the finger flat across the strings at one fret, make an open chord shape with the other three fingers just above it, and the whole shape becomes movable: slide it up or down and it plays a new chord with the same quality. Two shapes do most of the work, the E shape with its root on the 6th string and the A shape with its root on the 5th string, and each has a minor version that differs by a single finger.

1 F, the E shape at fret 1

Index finger across all six strings at fret 1. Middle finger on the 3rd string at fret 2, ring finger on the 5th string at fret 3, little finger on the 4th string at fret 3. The root, F, is the lowest note, on the 6th string.

2 Fm, the Em shape at fret 1

The same grip with the middle finger lifted. The 3rd string now sounds A♭ under the barre instead of A: the chord's 3rd has dropped a half step, and the chord has turned minor.

3 B♭, the A shape at fret 1

Index finger across the top five strings at fret 1, with the 6th string not played. The ring finger lies flat across the 4th, 3rd and 2nd strings at fret 3 (or use three fingers there if that's easier). The root, B♭, is on the 5th string.

4 B♭m, the Am shape at fret 1

Index barre at fret 1 across five strings, middle finger on the 2nd string at fret 2, ring and little fingers on the 4th and 3rd strings at fret 3. The 2nd string's D♭ is the minor 3rd that makes the chord minor.

Because the shape never changes, the fret your index finger is on, together with the string the root is on, names the chord. Learn the natural notes on the 6th and 5th strings (Part 1 shows them) and you can play every major and minor chord with these four grips:

Barre chords are hard at first, and almost everyone's first attempts buzz. A few things make them come sooner:

  • Roll the index finger slightly onto its bony outer edge, which presses the strings more evenly than the soft middle of the finger.
  • Keep the thumb behind the neck, roughly opposite the middle finger, and let the weight of your arm pull the finger back into the strings instead of squeezing with the thumb.
  • Press just behind the fret wire, where the least force is needed.
  • Strum one string at a time to find the one that buzzes, then adjust only the finger responsible.
  • Start around fret 5, where the strings are easier to press than at fret 1, then work down towards the nut.
  • Keep sessions short at first. A tired hand squeezes harder and sounds worse, so rest, and come back tomorrow.

Arpeggio shapes up the neck

An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time instead of all at once. Guitarists use arpeggios everywhere: picked patterns in ballads, the opening of countless songs, and solos that outline the chords underneath them. If you pick each string of a chord shape in turn, you already have an arpeggio. Its notes are the chord's root, 3rd and 5th, repeated in higher octaves, and because they're the chord's own notes, they always sound right over that chord.

To read the drawings in this section, start at the lowest dot on the thickest string and play every dot in order, string by string, up to the highest dot on the thinnest string, then come back down. The darkest, ringed dots are the root, so you can see where each octave of the arpeggio begins. The staff above the first drawing shows the same notes written out, and the labels under the staff say which job each note does.

The open position only works for one chord, but the arpeggio inside each barre shape moves wherever the shape goes. These two cover two octaves each without moving your hand, using one finger per fret: the index finger takes the lowest fret of the shape, and the other fingers take the frets above it.

1 The C arpeggio inside the E shape

Seven notes, root to root over two octaves: C → E → G → C → E → G → C. The index finger covers fret 7, the middle finger fret 8, the ring finger fret 9 and the little finger fret 10.

Because the root is on the 6th string, this pattern moves with the E-shape barre chord: start it at fret 3 for G, at fret 5 for A.

2 The C arpeggio inside the A shape

Six notes from the root on the 5th string: C → E → G → C → E → G. The index finger covers fret 2, the middle finger fret 3 and the ring or little finger fret 5.

The root is on the 5th string, so this pattern follows the A-shape barre chord: start it at fret 5 for D, at fret 7 for E.

Playing an arpeggio straight up and down is only the start. These are ways to practise it that also turn it into music:

  • Start from the 3rd or the 5th instead of the root, so the arpeggio begins on a different colour of the same chord.
  • Play it in a pattern, such as root, 3rd, 5th, 3rd, then the same from the next note up, to get the fingers moving in every direction.
  • Pick a progression such as C → Am → F → G and play each chord's arpeggio in the same area of the neck, changing when the chord changes. The notes that move are the ones that make each chord sound new.
  • Play the arpeggio slowly with a metronome, one note per click, then two, then three in a triplet feel.
  • In a solo, land on an arpeggio note, especially the root or the 3rd, whenever the chord changes. That one habit makes improvised lines sound connected to the chords.

Part 3: For the professional

Everything so far assumed standard tuning, but the six strings can be tuned to other notes, and Warbling Wren offers 16 tunings. Retuning changes every shape on the neck, and it can also change what the instrument wants to play: a tuning can make a chord ring with no fingers down, put a drone under every melody, or make heavy riffs fall under one finger. This part explains the tunings in the app, how they differ, and what each one invites you to try.

The tuning families

The 16 tunings fall into a few families. Within a family, the idea is the same and only the starting note differs, so once you understand one tuning in a family, you understand them all.

What the open strings sound like

The quickest way to understand a tuning is to look at its open strings. Each card below shows one tuning's six open strings only. The letters at the left are the strings' notes in that tuning, from the lowest string at the bottom to the highest at the top, and the coloured dots show the notes they ring when you strum without fretting anything.

1 Standard

Strings a fourth apart, with one third between the 3rd and 2nd strings. Strummed open, it isn't a chord, which is why almost every chord needs fingers down. Its strength is that scales and shapes fall evenly under the hand in every key.

2 Drop D

Only the 6th string changes, down to D. The bottom three strings now play D, A, D: a root, a fifth and an octave, the sound of a power chord, on open strings.

3 Open G

The open strings spell a G chord: G, B and D. Strum without fretting and you hear a full major chord, which is why slide players love it.

4 Open D

A D chord on the open strings, spread low and wide with three Ds. It sounds deeper and more spacious than Open G.

5 DADGAD

The open strings make Dsus4: D with a G in place of the 3rd. Because the chord is neither major nor minor, it sits comfortably under both, and open strings ring against almost any fretted note.

6 New Standard

Five strings a fifth apart, like a cello extended upward, with a minor third on top. The range is wider than standard tuning, and chords come out spread wide, more like a piano or an orchestra.

The same music in different tunings

The real difference shows when you play. A tuning that matches the music turns hard grips into easy ones; the cards below show two of the most useful cases, side by side with the standard-tuning version.

1 A G5 power chord in Standard

Two or three fingers: G on the 6th string at fret 3, then D and G two frets higher on the next two strings.

2 The same G5 in Drop D

One finger across the bottom three strings at fret 5. Slide that one finger anywhere and you have every power chord, which is why so much heavy rock is played in Drop D and Drop C.

3 C in Open G, with one finger

Because the open strings are a G chord, a single finger across fret 5 moves that chord up five half steps, to C. Fret 7 gives D, so the open strings, fret 5 and fret 7 are a complete I–IV–V.

4 D in Open G, two frets higher

The same one-finger barre at fret 7. Add a slide (a glass or metal tube worn on a finger) instead of the barre and you have the classic slide-guitar sound.

The trade-off is that a tuning built for one key or style makes others harder. In Open G, a minor chord or a chord outside the key needs new shapes, so players usually pick an open tuning to suit a particular song.

One chord, two tunings

Even a small change can transform a familiar chord. In Drop D, the open 6th string is D, so a Dm chord gains a low root on the 6th string and the open 5th string joins in too.

1 Dm in Standard tuning

Four strings only. The open 6th string, E, doesn't belong to the chord, and the open 5th string, A, is usually left out too, so the chord's lowest note is its root, D, on the open 4th string.

2 Dm in Drop D

The same fingers, but now all six strings ring: the dropped 6th string adds a low D, and the open A on the 5th string fills in the 5th.

Ideas to try with a new tuning

  • In Drop D, write a riff using only one-finger power chords on the bottom three strings, then add the open 6th string as a pedal note between them.
  • In Open G or Open D, play a melody on the top string while the lower open strings ring underneath like a drone.
  • In DADGAD, take any chord shape you know, move it around, and let the open strings ring: you'll find colours that standard tuning can't make.
  • If a song sits too high for your voice, try E♭ or D Standard: your chord shapes stay the same, and the whole song drops to a lower key.
  • Retune slowly and tune up to each note rather than down to it, so the strings hold their pitch. Tunings far below standard, such as C Standard, often feel better with heavier strings.
  • In Warbling Wren, switch tunings and watch every chord shape recalculate, then pick one tuning and explore it for a week.

How the app finds a shape

Warbling Wren works out every chord shape fresh for the tuning you pick, using the same rules a teacher would: every note of the chord must sound (a big chord may leave out its 5th), the chord's lowest note must be its bass note, no string in the middle may be skipped, at most four fingers, and the fretted notes must fit within four frets. Of all the shapes that pass, the one lowest on the neck with the fewest fingers wins. When the best shape is unusual, the diagram gets a ✦ tag.

Using it at the instrument

  • Learn the notes on the 6th string first, then the 5th. Say each note as you play it, up to fret 12 and back.
  • Use octave shapes to check yourself: find a note on the 6th string, then find it again two strings up and two frets along.
  • Pick one chord and play it in all five CAGED shapes, moving up the neck. Then do it with G.
  • Play the arpeggio inside each chord shape before you strum it.
  • Retune to Drop D for a song in D and listen to the low string ring under every chord.
