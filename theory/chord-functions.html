What Each Chord Does: Tonic, Subdominant and Dominant

Every chord in a key has a job: it feels like home, it moves away, or it builds tension that wants to resolve. Once you can hear those three jobs, every progression starts to make sense. This page explains chord functions from beginner to expert.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. Every example of home, away and tension on this page is made from them.

Part 1: The basics

First, a few words you'll need

  • Key. The "home base" of a song. A song in the key of C keeps coming back to C, and it feels finished when it lands there.
  • Scale. The seven notes a key is built from. The C major scale is C D E F G A B, all the white keys on a piano from C to C.
  • Half step and whole step. A half step is the smallest move on a piano: one key to the very next key, black or white (E to F, or C to C♯). On a guitar it's one fret. A whole step is two half steps (C to D), or two frets.
  • Chord. Three or more notes played together. The basic chord stacks every other note of the scale. Start on C, skip D, take E, skip F, take G, and you get C–E–G, the C major chord.
  • Roman numerals. Each note of the scale can be the bottom note (the root) of its own chord. Roman numerals number those chords by where the root sits in the scale: I is built on the 1st note, IV on the 4th, V on the 5th. Uppercase means a major chord (bright), and lowercase means a minor chord (darker). The charts above show all seven in C major.

Numerals let us describe a progression without naming a key. I – IV – V is C – F – G in C, and G – C – D in G.

Home: the tonic

I (or i in a minor key) is the chord the key is named after. In the key of C, that's the C chord. It sounds settled and at rest, like the full stop at the end of a sentence. Songs usually start here, and they almost always end here.

Two other chords can stand in for home: vi (Am in C) and iii (Em in C). Each shares two of its three notes with the home chord. C is C–E–G, Am is A–C–E, and Em is E–G–B. Because they sound so similar, they feel restful too, just a little less settled than the real thing.

Away: the subdominant

IV (F in C) and ii (Dm in C) take the song away from home. They don't sound tense, but they don't sound finished either. They make you feel the song is going somewhere.

F (F–A–C) and Dm (D–F–A) share two notes, F and A, which is why they can do the same job.

In a minor key the "away" chords are iv, ii° and VI. In A minor those are Dm, B° and F.

Tension: the dominant

V (G in C) is the chord that pulls hardest back to home. When you hear it, your ear expects the home chord next, and it feels satisfying when it arrives. There are two reasons for that pull:

  1. The leading tone. The G chord (G–B–D) contains B. B is just a half step below C, the home note, and a note a half step away wants to slide into its neighbour. B "leads" to C, which is where the name comes from.
  2. The 7th makes it stronger. Add one more note to G and you get G7 (G–B–D–F). Now B wants to go up to C, and F wants to go down to E. Those two notes, B and F, make the most unstable sound in music, called a tritone. When they move to C and E, notes of the C chord, the tension melts away. That move from tension to rest is the sound of a song "resolving".

Meet the chord: G7

G7 is the V chord with one more note stacked on top: F. All four notes are in the C major scale, so it belongs to the key. It's called a dominant 7th chord, because it's built on the dominant (V).

Part 2: Going deeper

Tension in a minor key

Build the v chord from the plain A minor scale (A B C D E F G) and you get Em (E–G–B). That's a weak pull, because G is a whole step below A, too far to lead into it.

So musicians usually raise that G by a half step to G♯. That turns Em into E major (E–G♯–B). Now G♯ sits a half step below A and leads strongly home. The A minor scale with that raised note is called harmonic minor. It's why so many minor-key songs use a major V chord.

Meet the chord: E

E major isn't built from the plain A minor scale, because of its G♯. It comes from A harmonic minor (A B C D E F G♯): the minor scale with its 7th note raised a half step.

Gentle tension: the chord a whole step below home

In a minor key, VII is the major chord built a whole step below home: G in A minor. It nudges you toward home without the strong pull of V, because there's no half step leading in. This chord is called the subtonic.

Rock and folk love this chord because it sounds open and unforced. Going VII → i (G → Am) feels like walking home instead of being pulled there.

Pointing at a different chord: secondary dominants

Every chord has its own "V", the chord that pulls toward it. Normally the only chord that gets pulled to is home. But you can borrow the V of any other chord to point the song toward it.

In C, the V chord is G. The chord that pulls toward G is D7 (D–F♯–A–C). Its F♯ is a half step below G, so it leads into G the same way B leads into C. We write it II7, or call it "the V of V". F♯ isn't in the C major scale, so D7 sounds like a surprise twist that then leads neatly to G, and G then leads home to C.

Meet the chord: D7

D7 (D–F♯–A–C) borrows F♯ from the G major scale, the key it points to. That one outside note is what makes it a secondary dominant: the V7 chord of G, written V7/V.

Chords from outside the key: borrowed chords

Sometimes a song uses a chord that isn't built from its own scale at all. It's borrowed from a close cousin of that scale.

The most famous example is ♭VII in a major key: B♭ in the key of C. The C major scale has B, not B♭. But change that one note, B to B♭, and you get a slightly different scale (C D E F G A B♭) called Mixolydian. B♭ (B♭–D–F) is built from that scale.

Meet the chord: B♭

B♭ (B♭–D–F) comes from C Mixolydian: the C major scale with its 7th lowered from B to B♭. The same chord also appears in C minor (C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭), which is why it feels at home in rock and blues.

A chord's name isn't its job

In the 12-bar blues, the home chord is C7, not plain C. C7 is the same type of chord as G7, the tension chord, yet in the blues it does the home job. Making even the home chord a little restless is what gives the blues its sound: the music never fully sits still.

Meet the chord: C7

C7 (C–E–G–B♭) is the C chord with a B♭ added. B♭ isn't in C major; in the blues it comes from the blues sound itself, where the 7th of every chord is lowered. The same chord shape is used for all three blues chords: C7, F7 and G7.

Part 3: For the professional

This part uses the language players and teachers use when they talk about chord functions. Every term is explained as it comes up.

Functional harmony

The idea that every chord does one of three jobs is called functional harmony. It was set out by the German theorist Hugo Riemann in the late 1800s, who named the three functions with letters still used today: T for tonic (home), S for subdominant (away) and D for dominant (tension). The usual order of the story is T → S → D → T.

FunctionIn C majorIn A minorWhy they belong together
Tonic (T)I C, vi Am, iii Emi Am, III CEach shares two of its three notes with the home chord (C or Am).
Subdominant (S)IV F, ii Dmiv Dm, ii° B°, VI FEach shares two notes with the chord built on the 4th note of the scale (F in C, Dm in A minor), the chord that leads away from home.
Dominant (D)V G, vii° B°V E, VII GThey contain the leading tone, a half step below home (B in C, G♯ in A minor). VII is the exception, explained below.

Notice that B° appears in both columns: as an away chord (ii° of A minor) it's subdominant, and as vii° of C major it's dominant. Function depends on the key, not the chord. In minor, VII (G) is listed as dominant even though it has no leading tone; some teachers call it subtonic instead, because its pull is so gentle.

Predominant chords

Many teachers call the subdominant group predominant chords, because their job is to come right before the dominant. The strongest predominant in jazz and classical music is ii (Dm in C), because its root falls a fifth to the root of V: one step around the circle of fifths. IV does the same job with a weaker root move (a step up to G).

Tonic substitutes and the deceptive cadence

Because vi and iii share two notes with I, they can take the tonic's place. The most famous use is the deceptive cadence, V → vi: the dominant sets up home, and the music lands on its stand-in instead. iii is a weaker substitute, because it also contains the leading tone (B), so it can sound like part of the dominant.

Secondary functions

Any chord in a key can be treated as a temporary home. A secondary dominant (V7/V) makes G sound like home for a moment; a secondary predominant goes one step further back, so Am7 becomes "the ii of V" in front of D7. This is called tonicisation: briefly treating another chord as the tonic without actually changing key.

Function in modal and popular music

Functional harmony describes most classical music, jazz standards and a lot of pop. Modal music works differently. In G Mixolydian (G A B C D E F), there's no leading tone at all, so there's no dominant in the classical sense: v is a minor chord (Dm). Home is established by repetition and by ♭VII → I moves instead. Many pop loops, such as vi–IV–I–V, also avoid a strong dominant → tonic move, so the home chord is suggested rather than declared.

Meet the chords: F and Dm

In G Mixolydian, the chords one step below and five steps above home are F (♭VII) and Dm (v). Neither contains F♯, so neither pulls to G the way a D7 chord would.

Using it at the instrument

  • Play I–IV–V–I slowly and name each chord's job out loud: home, away, tension, home. Then do the same with every progression you know.
  • Swap stand-ins. In any song, try replacing IV with ii, or I with vi, and listen to how the job stays the same while the colour changes.
  • Sing the leading tone. Play G7 and sing B, then let it rise to C as C arrives. That half step is the sound of the dominant.
  • On piano, find the common notes. Between C and Am only one note moves; between F and Dm two notes stay. Keep the shared notes still and move the rest to the nearest key.
  • On guitar, learn C, F and G in one spot on the neck, then add a 7th to the V chord (G7). You'll hear the extra pull immediately.
  • In a minor key, play Em → Am and then E → Am back to back. Hearing that one note change (G to G♯) is the quickest way to understand harmonic minor.
  • Add a secondary dominant to a song you know: before any G chord, try D7 first.
  • Try the rock ending B♭ → F → C at the end of a song instead of G → C, and listen to the difference between being pulled home and walking home.
