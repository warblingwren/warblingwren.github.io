What Each Chord Does: Tonic, Subdominant and Dominant

First, a few words you'll need

  • Key. The "home base" of a song. A song in the key of C keeps coming back to C, and it feels finished when it lands there.
  • Scale. The seven notes a key is built from. The C major scale is C D E F G A B, all the white keys on a piano from C to C.
  • Half step and whole step. A half step is the smallest move on a piano: one key to the very next key, black or white (E to F, or C to C♯). On a guitar it's one fret. A whole step is two half steps (C to D), or two frets.
  • Chord. Three or more notes played together. The basic chord stacks every other note of the scale. Start on C, skip D, take E, skip F, take G, and you get C – E – G, the C major chord.
  • Roman numerals. Each note of the scale can be the bottom note (the root) of its own chord. Roman numerals number those chords by where the root sits in the scale: I is built on the 1st note, IV on the 4th, V on the 5th. Uppercase means a major chord (bright), and lowercase means a minor chord (darker). In the key of C:
I ii iii IV V vi vii°
C Dm Em F G Am B°

Numerals let us describe a progression without naming a key. I – IV – V is C – F – G in C, and G – C – D in G.

Every chord has a job

Listen to almost any song and the chords tell the same little story: home, then away, then tension, then back home. Each chord in a key plays one of those parts.

Home: the tonic

I (or i in a minor key) is the chord the key is named after. In the key of C, that's the C chord. It sounds settled and at rest, like the full stop at the end of a sentence. Songs usually start here, and they almost always end here.

Two other chords can stand in for home: vi (Am in C) and iii (Em in C). Each shares two of its three notes with the home chord. C is C–E–G, Am is A–C–E, and Em is E–G–B. Because they sound so similar, they feel restful too, just a little less settled than the real thing.

Away: the subdominant

IV (F in C) and ii (Dm in C) take the song away from home. They don't sound tense, but they don't sound finished either. They make you feel the song is going somewhere.

F (F–A–C) and Dm (D–F–A) share two notes, F and A, which is why they can do the same job.

In a minor key the "away" chords are iv, ii° and VI. In A minor those are Dm, B diminished and F.

Tension: the dominant

V (G in C) is the chord that pulls hardest back to home. When you hear it, your ear expects the home chord next, and it feels satisfying when it arrives.

There are two reasons for that pull:

  1. The leading tone. The G chord (G–B–D) contains B. B is just a half step below C, the home note, and a note a half step away wants to slide into its neighbour. B "leads" to C, which is where the name comes from.
  2. The 7th makes it stronger. Add one more note to G and you get G7 (G–B–D–F). Now B wants to go up to C, and F wants to go down to E. Those two notes, B and F, make the most unstable sound in music, called a tritone. When they move to C and E, notes of the C chord, the tension melts away. That move from tension to rest is the sound of a song "resolving".

vii° (B diminished, B–D–F in C) contains those same two restless notes, B and F, so it pulls home almost as strongly as V.

Tension in a minor key

Build the V chord from the plain A minor scale (A B C D E F G) and you get Em (E–G–B). That's a weak pull, because G is a whole step below A, too far to lead into it.

So musicians usually raise that G by a half step to G♯. That turns Em into E major (E–G♯–B). Now G♯ sits a half step below A and leads strongly home. The A minor scale with that raised note is called harmonic minor. It's why so many minor-key songs use a major V chord.

Gentle tension: the chord a whole step below home

In a minor key, VII is the major chord built a whole step below home: G in A minor. It nudges you toward home without the strong pull of V, because there's no half step leading in.

Rock and folk love this chord because it sounds open and unforced. Going VII → i (G → Am) feels like walking home instead of being pulled there.

Pointing at a different chord: secondary dominants

Every chord has its own "V", the chord that pulls toward it. Normally the only chord that gets pulled to is home. But you can borrow the V of any other chord to point the song toward it.

In C, the V chord is G. The chord that pulls toward G is D7 (D–F♯–A–C). Its F♯ is a half step below G, so it leads into G the same way B leads into C. We write it II7, or call it "the V of V". F♯ isn't in the C major scale, so D7 sounds like a surprise twist that then leads neatly to G, and G then leads home to C.

Chords from outside the key: borrowed chords

Sometimes a song uses a chord that isn't built from its own scale at all. It's borrowed from a close cousin of that scale.

The most famous example is ♭VII in a major key: B♭ in the key of C. The C major scale has B, not B♭. But change that one note, B to B♭, and you get a slightly different scale (C D E F G A B♭) called Mixolydian. B♭ (B♭–D–F) is built from that scale.

Going B♭ → F → C (♭VII – IV – I) gives a big, open sound with no tension at all, because there's no leading tone pulling. It's all over classic rock.

One more thing: a chord's name isn't its job

In the 12-bar blues, the home chord is C7, not plain C. C7 is the same type of chord as G7, the tension chord, yet in the blues it does the home job. Making even the home chord a little restless is what gives the blues its sound: the music never fully sits still.
