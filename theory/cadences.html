Cadences: How Music Ends a Phrase

Some chord changes sound like the end of a sentence. Some sound like a comma, others sound like a question, and some sound like a plot twist. Those endings are called cadences, and they're how music breathes. This page explains Cadence from beginner to expert.

Part 1: The basics

What is a cadence?

A cadence is the last two chords of a musical phrase: the moment the music pauses or stops. The word comes from the Latin cadere, "to fall", because a cadence is where the music settles.

Music is built from phrases, like sentences in speech. Every phrase ends somewhere, and the last two chords decide how that ending feels: finished, unfinished, surprising, or calm.

First, a few words you'll need to understand

  • Key. The home base of a song. A song in the key of C keeps coming back to C.
  • Roman numerals. Chords are numbered by the order the note appears in the scale they're built on. For example, in the key of C: I = C, ii = Dm, iii = Em, IV = F, V = G, vi = Am. Uppercase is a major chord, lowercase is minor.
  • Home, away and tension. I is home (the tonic). IV is away (the subdominant). V is tension (the dominant): it pulls hard back to home.

Part 2: Going deeper

Authentic cadence (V → I): the full stop

The "full stop" is the strongest, most final ending in music. In the key of C it's G → C. Two things make it so convincing:

  1. The leading tone. The G chord (G–B–D) contains B, which is just a half step below C. A note a half step away naturally wants to slide into its neighbour, and B "leads" right into C, the home note.
  2. The fall of a fifth. The bass drops from G to C, which is exactly a fifth down. That's the strongest root move there is: one step counterclockwise around the circle of fifths, toward the home key.

Adding the 7th

Next if you add a 7th to the V chord, you get G7 (G–B–D–F), which makes the pull even stronger because now, F naturally steps down to E (one half step down), while B naturally wants to step up to C(one half step up). Those two restless notes, B and F, together form a tritone, and when they land on C and E the tension disappears. Most pieces of classical music, and a wide array of pop and jazz arrangements, end this way.

Minor Keys

In a minor key, the V chord is usually made major so it keeps its leading tone. For example, in A minor Am (A–C–E), use E major (E–G♯–B) instead of E minor (E–G–B) because G♯ E major is a half step below A, and G isn't, and therefore the E major naturally wants to slide to the A.

Plagal cadence (IV → I): the "Amen"

The IV → I cadence in the key of C is F → C. It's often called the Amen cadence because it's the two chords sung on "A–men" at the end of many hymns.

There's no leading tone here, so there's no strong pull. Instead the F chord (F–A–C) and the C chord (C–E–G) share the note C, which stays put while the others settle down by step: F to E, and A to G. The result is warm, calm and final ending that is softer than the authentic ending (V → I). The A-men often comes after an authentic cadence, as a gentle close to a song.

Variations

A favourite variation is the minor plagal cadence. Using the example: Fm → C in the key of C. The minor IV chord Fm chord (F–A♭–C) brings in A♭, which sinks a half step down to G. It's a bittersweet, wistful sound, heard everywhere from gospel to film music.

Half cadence (→ V): the comma

Any phrase that stops on the V chord. In the key of C use any of C → G , F → G or Dm → G. This pauses the music on the tension chord, so your left to hear the question but not the answer. It sounds open and unfinished, but the listener knows there's more coming.

Question & Answer

Half cadences usually sit in the middle of a song. A very common pattern is two phrases, the first ending on a half cadence (the question) and the second ending on an authentic cadence (the answer).

Deceptive cadence (V → vi): the plot twist

This happens when the V chord sets up home, but the music goes somewhere else. In the key of C try G → Am. Your ear expected C, and it got Am instead.

This catches your ear because Am (A–C–E) shares two notes with C (C–E–G). So, it's close enough to home to make sense, but it's slightly darker (because it's a minor), so the ending is postponed. The leading tone B still rises to C (one half step up), as expected; it's the bass that moves up a step to A instead of falling to C. Composers use the deceptive cadence to keep a song going, to stretch a phrase before the real ending, or for an emotional turn. It's called the interrupted cadence in British usage.

The Minor

In a minor key the deceptive cadence is V → VI which is E → F in the key A minor.

Phrygian cadence and the Andalusian cadence: the dramatic pause

These are minor-key endings that land on the V chord, so they're a special kind of half cadence, with a strong character of their own.

  • The iv → V is known as the Phrygian cadence. In the key of A minor, try the cadence Dm → E. Note how the Dm chord has F in the bass, and the bass sinks a half step from F to E while a voice above rises from D to E. That half-step fall onto the last chord give the chord an ancient and solemn sound. The name comes from the old Phrygian mode, characterized by closing melodies with the same half-step drop.
  • i → VII → VI → V is known as the Andalusian cadence. In the key of A minor, the run would be Am → G → F → E. This is a four-chord staircase straight down, landing on E major. You'll find this cadence throughout the sounds of flamenco and of Spanish folk music, and it shows up in rock and pop too. It ends with the same F → E half-step drop as the Phrygian cadence, which is why the two are related.

Hanging on a Note

Because they end on V, both leave you hanging. In flamenco, that final E major can even sound like a home chord in its own right.

The Soft Blues Turnaround

In 12 bar blues, notice the bars 9 to 11 that go V7 → IV7 → I7. That last step that goes IV7 → I7 is a plagal move, that softens the V7's pull right before the turnaround.

Part 3: For the professional

Perfect and imperfect authentic cadences

Not every V → I cadence is equally final.

Perfect Authentic Cadence (PAC) A perfect authentic cadence (PAC) needs both chords in root position and the tonic in the top voice on the final chord.
Imperfect Authentic Cadence (AAC) If either chord is inverted, the melody ends on the 3rd or 5th, or vii° stands in for V, it's an imperfect authentic cadence (IAC), which is conclusive but less emphatic.

The resulting nuance: a PAC closes a section, an IAC closes a phrase and keeps things moving.

The cadential six-four

Commonly, the V chord of a strong cadence is often decorated by a I chord over the dominant bass (for example, I6/4 which uses C/G in the key of C). This causes upper voices to fall by a step to the V chord (C → B and E → D), and therefore the V chord resolves to the I chord.

This allows six-four to be used as an embellishment of the V chord rather than a tonic chord, so the cadence is still V → I with a delayed arrival of the dominant.

Phrase structure: the period

Two phrases in a call-and-answer pair form a period. The first (antecedent) ends with a weaker cadence, usually half or IAC. The second (consequent) answers with a PAC. The strength of each cadence is what makes the pair sound like a question and answer. Delaying the PAC with deceptive or evaded cadences is a core technique for extending phrases.

Evaded and abandoned cadences

An evaded cadence sets up a V → I resolution, and then avoids it ... most often with V → I6 (C/E in the key of C) , V → IV6 , or V7 → V4/2 → I6. The listener hears the ending coming, and the music sidesteps it at the last moment. This technique is related to the deceptive cadence, but the harmony usually stays closer to tonic, and the phrase is typically repeated and then closed properly.

Deceptive targets beyond vi

In this cadence, V → vi is the textbook deception, but the V can resolve to many chords that naturally push the leading tone to the tonic note.

For example, in the key of in C, the cadence V → IV6, V → ♭VI produces the transition G → A♭, which has a darker twist borrowed from C minor).

Taking it one step further, the transition V → to any secondary dominant such as V7/IV (G7 → C7). This makes the music turn away from home immediately.

Phrygian half cadence voice leading

The classic Phrygian half cadence is iv6 → V in minor. For example, in the key of A minor you get Dm/F → E. In this cadence, the bass falls a half step, scale degree ♭6 to 5 (F → E). The top voice rises a whole step, 4 to 5 (D → E), in contrary motion, often by an octave or a unison.

Phrygian half cadence voice leading is a staple of the Baroque, frequently ending a slow movement before a fast finale in the same key. Its half-step descent onto the final is a relic of the Phrygian mode's ♭2 → 1 close, re-interpreted inside tonal minor.

The Andalusian cadence, analysed

This is a pattern used since the Renaissance.

i – ♭VII – ♭VI – V (speficically in the key of A minor Am – G – F – E) harmonises a descending tetrachord in the bass, A – G – F – E.

The listener hears the nuance in A minor, ending on a Phrygian half cadence; followed by a modal transition, closer to flamenco practice; before hearing E as the tonic of an E Phrygian (or "Phrygian dominant") mode. The the cadence is brought to a full close onto E, with F → E as the defining ♭2 → 1 step.

Backdoor cadence iv7 → ♭VII7 → I, or ♭VII7 → I Fm7 → B♭7 → C Jazz's alternative to ii–V–I. B♭7 shares notes with the minor iv and reaches I from the "wrong" side of the circle.
Picardy third A minor-key piece ending on a major tonic chord E → A instead of E → Am Standard in the Renaissance and Baroque, and still used today for a moment of light at the end.

Cadence and meter

Where the final chord falls in the bar matters as much as which chord it is. A cadence landing on a strong beat sounds decisive. One landing on a weak beat, or with the resolution delayed by a suspension (4–3 or 9–8 over the final chord), sounds softer and more open-ended. Harmonic rhythm usually speeds up into a cadence, with chords changing faster before the arrival, which adds to the sense of an ending.

Loops and the missing cadence

A lot of popular music is built on repeating loops with no true cadence inside the loop. Each chord change works as a weak cadence, and the "home" chord is negotiated rather than declared. For example, the vi–IV–I–V and I–V–vi–IV rotations are the same four chords, yet they feel minor or major depending on where the loop starts and which chord lands on the strongest beats.

Songwriters often break a loop with a real V → I or a held IV → I at the end of a chorus, precisely because the contrast makes the ending land.
