Cadences: How Music Ends a Phrase

Some chord changes sound like the end of a sentence. Some sound like a comma, some like a question, and some like a plot twist. Those endings are called cadences, and they're how music breathes. This page starts from zero and works up to the ideas professionals use. Read as far as you like.

Part 1: The basics

What is a cadence?

A cadence is the last two chords of a musical phrase: the moment the music pauses or stops. The word comes from the Latin cadere, "to fall", because a cadence is where the music settles.

Music is built from phrases, like sentences in speech. Every phrase ends somewhere, and the last two chords decide how that ending feels: finished, unfinished, surprising, or calm.

A few words you'll need

  • Key. The home base of a song. A song in C keeps coming back to C.
  • Roman numerals. Chords are numbered by the scale note they're built on. In the key of C: I = C, ii = Dm, iii = Em, IV = F, V = G, vi = Am. Uppercase is a major chord, lowercase is minor.
  • Home, away and tension. I is home (the tonic). IV is away (the subdominant). V is tension (the dominant): it pulls hard back to home.

Part 2: Going deeper

Authentic cadence (V → I): the full stop

The strongest, most final ending in music. In C it's G → C. Two things make it so convincing:

  1. The leading tone. The G chord (G–B–D) contains B, just a half step below C. A note a half step away wants to slide into its neighbour, and B "leads" right into C, the home note.
  2. The fall of a fifth. The bass drops from G to C, a fifth down. That's the strongest root move there is: one step counterclockwise around the circle of fifths, toward home.

Add a 7th to the V chord, G7 (G–B–D–F), and the pull gets stronger. Now F also wants to step down to E, while B steps up to C. Those two restless notes, B and F, together form a tritone, and when they land on C and E the tension disappears. Most pieces of classical music, and a huge amount of pop and jazz, end this way.

In a minor key, the V chord is usually made major so it keeps its leading tone. In A minor, E major (E–G♯–B) instead of E minor (E–G–B): G♯ is a half step below A, and G isn't.

Plagal cadence (IV → I): the "Amen"

In C it's F → C. It's often called the Amen cadence because it's the two chords sung on "A–men" at the end of many hymns.

There's no leading tone here, so there's no strong pull. Instead the F chord (F–A–C) and the C chord (C–E–G) share the note C, which stays put while the others settle down by step: F to E, A to G. The result is warm, calm and final, but softer than V → I. It often comes after an authentic cadence, as a gentle extra ending.

A favourite variation is the minor plagal cadence: Fm → C in the key of C. The minor IV chord brings in A♭, which sinks a half step down to G. It's a bittersweet, wistful sound, heard everywhere from gospel to film music.

Half cadence (→ V): the comma

Any phrase that stops on the V chord. In C: C → G, F → G or Dm → G. The music pauses on the tension chord, so you hear the question but not the answer. It sounds open and unfinished: you know there's more coming.

Half cadences usually sit in the middle of a song. A very common pattern is two phrases, the first ending on a half cadence (the question) and the second ending on an authentic cadence (the answer).

Deceptive cadence (V → vi): the plot twist

The V chord sets up home, but the music goes somewhere else. In C: G → Am. Your ear expected C, and it got Am instead.

It works because Am (A–C–E) shares two notes with C (C–E–G). It's close enough to home to make sense, but it's minor and darker, so the ending is postponed. The leading tone B still rises to C, as expected; it's the bass that moves up a step to A instead of falling to C. Composers use the deceptive cadence to keep a song going, to stretch a phrase before the real ending, or for an emotional turn. It's called the interrupted cadence in British usage. In a minor key it's V → VI: E → F in A minor.

Phrygian cadence and the Andalusian cadence: the dramatic pause

These are minor-key endings that land on the V chord, so they're a special kind of half cadence, with a strong character of their own.

  • Phrygian cadence (iv → V). In A minor: Dm → E. Usually the Dm chord has F in the bass, and the bass sinks a half step from F to E while a voice above rises from D to E. That half-step fall onto the last chord sounds ancient and solemn. The name comes from the old Phrygian mode, whose melodies close with the same half-step drop.
  • Andalusian cadence (i → VII → VI → V). In A minor: Am → G → F → E. A four-chord staircase straight down, landing on E major. It's the sound of flamenco and of Spanish folk music, and it shows up in rock and pop too. It ends with the same F → E half-step drop as the Phrygian cadence, which is why the two are related.

Because they end on V, both leave you hanging. In flamenco, that final E major can even sound like a home chord in its own right.

Part 3: For the professional

Perfect and imperfect authentic cadences

Not every V → I is equally final. A perfect authentic cadence (PAC) needs both chords in root position and the tonic in the top voice on the final chord. If either chord is inverted, the melody ends on the 3rd or 5th, or vii° stands in for V, it's an imperfect authentic cadence (IAC), which is conclusive but less emphatic. Classical form leans on the difference: a PAC closes a section, an IAC closes a phrase and keeps things moving.

The cadential six-four

In common-practice music, the V of a strong cadence is often decorated by a I chord over the dominant bass (I6/4: C/G in C). The upper voices then fall by step to V (C → B, E → D), and V resolves to I. Most theorists read the six-four as an embellishment of V rather than a tonic chord, so the cadence is still V → I with a delayed arrival of the dominant.

Phrase structure: the period

Two phrases in a call-and-answer pair form a period. The first (antecedent) ends with a weaker cadence, usually half or IAC. The second (consequent) answers with a PAC. The strength of each cadence is what makes the pair sound like a question and answer. Delaying the PAC with deceptive or evaded cadences is a core technique for extending phrases.

Evaded and abandoned cadences

An evaded cadence sets up V → I, then avoids it, most often with V → I6 (C/E), V → IV6, or V7 → V4/2 → I6. The listener hears the ending coming, and the music sidesteps it at the last moment. It's a cousin of the deceptive cadence, but the harmony usually stays closer to tonic, and the phrase is typically repeated and then closed properly.

Deceptive targets beyond vi

V → vi is the textbook deception, but V can resolve to many chords that keep the leading tone's expected step to the tonic note: V → IV6, V → ♭VI (G → A♭ in C, a darker twist borrowed from C minor), or V → a secondary dominant such as V7/IV (G7 → C7) that turns away from home immediately.

Phrygian half cadence voice leading

The classic Phrygian half cadence is iv6 → V in minor: in A minor, Dm/F → E. The bass falls a half step, scale degree ♭6 to 5 (F → E). The top voice rises a whole step, 4 to 5 (D → E), in contrary motion, often by an octave or a unison. It's a staple of the Baroque, frequently ending a slow movement before a fast finale in the same key. Its half-step descent onto the final is a relic of the Phrygian mode's ♭2 → 1 close, re-interpreted inside tonal minor.

The Andalusian cadence, analysed

i – ♭VII – ♭VI – V (Am – G – F – E) harmonises a descending tetrachord in the bass, A – G – F – E, a pattern used since the Renaissance. Tonal analysis hears it in A minor, ending on a Phrygian half cadence. Modal analysis, closer to flamenco practice, hears E as the tonic of an E Phrygian (or "Phrygian dominant") mode. Then the cadence is a full close onto E, with F → E as the defining ♭2 → 1 step. Both readings are valid. Which one wins depends on where the music comes to rest.

Modal and popular cadences

  • Mixolydian cadence (♭VII → I). B♭ → C in C. A rising whole step with no leading tone: bright and final, without the classical push. A rock staple.
  • Aeolian cadence (♭VI → ♭VII → I). A♭ → B♭ → C in C. Two borrowed chords climbing by step into a major tonic: triumphant and widely used in rock and in video game music.
  • Backdoor cadence (iv7 → ♭VII7 → I, or ♭VII7 → I). Fm7 → B♭7 → C in C. Jazz's alternative to ii–V–I. B♭7 shares notes with the minor iv and reaches I from the "wrong" side of the circle.
  • Picardy third. A minor-key piece ending on a major tonic chord (E → A instead of E → Am). Standard in the Renaissance and Baroque, and still used today for a moment of light at the end.

Cadence and meter

Where the final chord falls in the bar matters as much as which chord it is. A cadence landing on a strong beat sounds decisive. One landing on a weak beat, or with the resolution delayed by a suspension (4–3 or 9–8 over the final chord), sounds softer and more open-ended. Harmonic rhythm usually speeds up into a cadence, with chords changing faster before the arrival, which adds to the sense of an ending.

Loops and the missing cadence

Much popular music is built on repeating loops (I–V–vi–IV, vi–IV–I–V) with no true cadence inside the loop. Each chord change works as a weak cadence, and the "home" chord is negotiated rather than declared. The vi–IV–I–V and I–V–vi–IV rotations are the same four chords, yet they feel minor or major depending on where the loop starts and which chord lands on the strongest beats. Songwriters often break a loop with a real V → I or a held IV → I at the end of a chorus, precisely because the contrast makes the ending land.
