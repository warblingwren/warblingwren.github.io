Cadences: How Music Ends a Phrase

Some chord changes sound like the end of a sentence. Some sound like a comma, others sound like a question, and some sound like a plot twist. Those endings are called cadences, and they're how music breathes. This page explains cadences from beginner to expert.

The chords we'll use

Every example on this page uses the chords of two scales: C major and A minor. They share the same seven notes, all the white keys on a piano (C D E F G A B), so you can play every example without a single sharp or flat until we deliberately add one.

Each chord below is built on one note of the scale, and the badge above it shows its position in the key (its Roman numeral). Take a minute to find these chords on your piano or guitar. Every cadence on this page is made from them.

Part 1: The basics

What is a cadence?

A cadence is the last two chords of a musical phrase: the moment the music pauses or stops. The word comes from the Latin cadere, "to fall", because a cadence is where the music settles.

Music is built from phrases, like sentences in speech. Every phrase ends somewhere, and the last two chords decide how that ending feels: finished, unfinished, surprising or calm.

First, a few words you'll need

  • Key. The home base of a song. A song in the key of C keeps coming back to C.
  • Roman numerals. Chords are numbered by the scale note they're built on. In the key of C: I = C, ii = Dm, iii = Em, IV = F, V = G, vi = Am. Uppercase is a major chord, lowercase is minor.
  • Home, away and tension. I is home (the tonic). IV is away (the subdominant). V is tension (the dominant): it pulls hard back to home.

Part 2: Going deeper

Authentic cadence (V → I): the full stop

The full stop is the strongest, most final ending in music. In the key of C it's G → C. Two things make it so convincing:

  1. The leading tone. The G chord (G–B–D) contains B, which is just a half step below C. A note a half step away naturally wants to slide into its neighbour, and B "leads" right into C, the home note.
  2. The fall of a fifth. The bass drops from G to C, exactly a fifth down. That's the strongest root move there is: one step counterclockwise around the circle of fifths, toward the home key.

Adding the 7th

Add a 7th to the V chord and you get G7 (G–B–D–F). That makes the pull even stronger, because now F naturally steps down to E (one half step down) while B steps up to C (one half step up). Those two restless notes, B and F, together form a tritone, and when they land on C and E the tension disappears. Most classical music, and a wide range of pop and jazz, ends this way.

Meet the chord: G7

G7 is the V chord with one more note stacked on top: F. All four notes are in the C major scale, so it belongs to the key. It's called a dominant 7th chord, and you'll meet it in almost every style of music.

Minor keys

In a minor key, the V chord is usually made major so it keeps its leading tone. In A minor, the home chord is Am (A–C–E). Built from the plain scale, the v chord would be Em (E–G–B), but musicians usually play E (E–G♯–B) instead. G♯ is a half step below A, so it pulls into A the same way B pulls into C. G is a whole step away, and doesn't pull.

Meet the chord: E

E major (E–G♯–B) isn't built from the plain A minor scale, because of its G♯. It comes from the harmonic minor scale: A minor with its 7th note raised a half step (A B C D E F G♯). That one raised note is what gives minor keys their strong pull home.

Plagal cadence (IV → I): the "Amen"

The IV → I cadence in the key of C is F → C. It's often called the Amen cadence, because it's the two chords sung on "A–men" at the end of many hymns.

There's no leading tone here, so there's no strong pull. Instead the F chord (F–A–C) and the C chord (C–E–G) share the note C, which stays put while the others settle down by step: F to E, and A to G. The result is a warm, calm ending that is softer than the authentic ending (V → I). The Amen often comes after an authentic cadence, as a gentle close to a song.

The minor plagal cadence

A favourite variation is the minor plagal cadence: Fm → C in the key of C. The minor iv chord, Fm (F–A♭–C), brings in A♭, which sinks a half step down to G. It's a bittersweet, wistful sound, heard everywhere from gospel to film music.

Meet the chord: Fm

Fm isn't in the C major scale, because of its A♭. It's borrowed from C minor (C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭), the minor scale with the same home note. Borrowing a chord from the minor version of your key is a quick way to add a touch of sadness.

Question and answer

Half cadences usually sit in the middle of a song. A very common pattern is two phrases: the first ends on a half cadence (the question), and the second ends on an authentic cadence (the answer).

Deceptive cadence (V → vi): the plot twist

This happens when the V chord sets up home, but the music goes somewhere else. In the key of C, try G → Am. Your ear expects C, and it gets Am instead.

It catches your ear because Am (A–C–E) shares two notes with C (C–E–G). It's close enough to home to make sense, but it's minor and darker, so the ending is postponed. The leading tone B still rises to C as expected; it's the bass that moves up a step to A instead of falling to C. Composers use the deceptive cadence to keep a song going, to stretch a phrase before the real ending, or for an emotional turn. In British usage it's called the interrupted cadence.

Phrygian cadence and the Andalusian cadence: the dramatic pause

These are minor-key endings that land on the V chord, so they're a special kind of half cadence, with a strong character of their own.

  • iv → V is the Phrygian cadence. In A minor, it's Dm → E, usually played with F in the bass, written Dm/F. The bass sinks a half step from F to E while a note above rises from D to E. That half-step fall onto the last chord gives it an ancient, solemn sound. The name comes from the old Phrygian mode, whose melodies close with the same half-step drop.
  • i → VII → VI → V is the Andalusian cadence. In A minor, it's Am → G → F → E: a four-chord staircase straight down, landing on E major. You'll hear it throughout flamenco and Spanish folk music, and it shows up in rock and pop too. It ends with the same F → E half-step drop as the Phrygian cadence, which is why the two are related.

Meet the chord: Dm/F

A chord name with a slash means "this chord, with this note in the bass". Dm/F is a Dm chord (D–F–A) with F as its lowest note. Same three notes, rearranged, all from the A minor scale. Putting F in the bass is what creates the half-step slide down to E.

Part 3: For the professional

This part uses the language players and teachers use when they talk about cadences. Every term is explained as it comes up.

Perfect and imperfect authentic cadences

Not every V → I cadence is equally final. Two details decide how final it sounds: which note is in the bass (the lowest note), and which note is on top (the melody).

CadenceWhat it needsHow it sounds
Perfect authentic cadence (PAC)Both chords in root position (the root is the lowest note: G in the bass for G, C for C), and the melody ends on the home note, C.As final as music gets.
Imperfect authentic cadence (IAC)Anything less: one chord has a different note in the bass, the melody ends on E or G instead of C, or vii° (B°) stands in for V.Finished, but less emphatic.

The difference matters in longer music: a PAC closes a whole section, while an IAC closes a phrase and keeps things moving.

The cadential six-four

Before the V chord of a strong cadence, composers often play a C chord with G in the bass: C/G, written I6/4 in Roman numerals. The notes above then fall by step into the G chord (C → B and E → D), and G resolves to C as usual: C/G → G → C.

What do C/G and I6/4 mean?

C/G is a C chord (C–E–G) with G as the lowest note. The slash names the bass note. Classical musicians write the same chord as I6/4: the I chord, with the numbers 6 and 4 describing the notes above the bass. Counting up from G, C is a 4th above and E is a 6th above. A chord with its 5th in the bass is called second inversion.

Even though it contains the notes of C, the cadential six-four doesn't sound like home. With G in the bass it sounds like G waiting to happen, which is why it's treated as a decoration of V: the cadence is still V → I, with the arrival of the V delayed for drama.

Phrase structure: the period

Two phrases in a call-and-answer pair form a period. The first phrase (the antecedent) ends with a weaker cadence, usually a half cadence or an IAC. The second phrase (the consequent) answers with a PAC. The difference in strength is what makes the pair sound like a question and its answer. Holding back the PAC with deceptive or evaded cadences is a core technique for making phrases longer.

Evaded cadences

An evaded cadence sets up a V → I resolution, then sidesteps it at the last moment. The listener hears the ending coming, and the music slips past it. Three common ways to do it in C:

  • G → C/E: C arrives, but with E in the bass, so it doesn't sound settled.
  • G → F/A: the music steps back to an "away" chord, F with A in the bass.
  • G7 → G7/F → C/E: the bass slips from G down to F, then to E, delaying home by one more chord.

It's related to the deceptive cadence, but the harmony usually stays closer to home, and the phrase is typically repeated and then closed properly.

Reading inversion numbers

Classical Roman numerals add small numbers to show which note is in the bass. These are the ones on this page:

  • I6 = the I chord with its 3rd in the bass: C/E. Called first inversion.
  • IV6 = the IV chord with its 3rd in the bass: F/A.
  • I6/4 = the I chord with its 5th in the bass: C/G. Called second inversion.
  • V4/2 = the V7 chord with its 7th in the bass: G7/F. Called third inversion.

Deceptive endings beyond vi

V → vi is the textbook deception, but the V chord can move to many chords that still let the leading tone B rise to C. Two favourites in C:

  • V → ♭VI: G → A♭, a darker twist borrowed from C minor.
  • V7 → V7/IV: G7 → C7. C7 is home with an extra B♭ that points straight to F, so the music turns away from home the moment it arrives.

Meet the chords: A♭ and C7

A♭ (A♭–C–E♭) comes from the C minor scale (C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭), like Fm earlier. C7 (C–E–G–B♭) adds B♭ to the C chord. B♭ isn't in C major; it belongs to F major, so C7 works as the V7 chord of F and points the music toward it. A chord that points at a chord other than home is called a secondary dominant, written V7/IV ("V7 of IV").

Phrygian half cadence voice leading

The classic Phrygian half cadence is iv6 → V in a minor key: Dm/F → E in A minor. Number the notes of the A minor scale from 1 (A) to 7 (G). The bass falls a half step from the 6th note to the 5th (F → E). At the same time the top note rises a whole step from the 4th note to the 5th (D → E). The two lines move in opposite directions (contrary motion) and meet on E, an octave apart.

This ending was a favourite in the Baroque era (roughly 1600 to 1750). Composers often used it to end a slow middle movement before a fast finale in the same key. Its half-step fall onto the final note is borrowed from the old Phrygian mode, where melodies closed by stepping down from the 2nd note to the 1st.

The Andalusian cadence, two ways

i – ♭VII – ♭VI – V (Am – G – F – E in A minor) harmonises a bass line that walks straight down four notes: A – G – F – E. This pattern has been used since the Renaissance, and it can be heard in two different ways:

  • In A minor. Home is Am, and the progression ends on its V chord, E. That makes the last step a Phrygian-style half cadence: an open ending that wants to go back to Am.
  • In E Phrygian. Flamenco players often hear E itself as home. In that reading the progression doesn't pause on E, it arrives there, and the final F → E step is the defining half-step close of the Phrygian mode. When E is major, as it is here, the scale is called Phrygian dominant.

Both readings are valid. Which one you hear depends on where the music comes to rest.

Cadence and meter

Where the final chord falls in the bar matters as much as which chord it is. A cadence that lands on a strong beat (beat 1) sounds decisive. One that lands on a weak beat sounds softer and more open-ended.

Composers can also soften an ending with a suspension: one note of the previous chord is held over into the final chord, clashing for a moment before it steps down into place. Over a C chord, for example, F can hang on before falling to E: that's a "4–3" suspension, because the note falls from the 4th above the bass to the 3rd.

The speed of the chord changes, called harmonic rhythm, usually picks up just before a cadence. Chords changing faster before the arrival adds to the sense of an ending.

Loops and the missing cadence

A lot of popular music is built on repeating loops with no true cadence inside them. Each chord change works as a weak cadence, and the home chord is suggested rather than declared. For example, vi–IV–I–V and I–V–vi–IV are the same four chords, yet they feel minor or major depending on where the loop starts and which chord lands on the strongest beats.

Songwriters often break a loop with a real V → I or a held IV → I at the end of a chorus, precisely because the contrast makes the ending land.
